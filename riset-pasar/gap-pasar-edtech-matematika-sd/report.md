### Ruangguru

<a id="ruangguru"></a>
**Basic Info**

- **name**: Ruangguru
- **category**: competitor_product
- **origin_region**: Indonesia (Jakarta-based; also operates in Vietnam and Thailand)
- **target_age**: > Kindergarten through Grade 12 (roughly ages 5-18), plus alumni/adults. Elementary (SD, Grades 1-6, ages 6-12) is served by 'ruangbelajar SD' and the separate 'Ruangguru For Kids' product line, so Grade 4 math is explicitly in scope.
- **delivery_mode**: > hybrid - primarily an online app/web platform (ruangbelajar video + quizzes, AiRIS AI chat, Roboguru via app/web/WhatsApp), but with offline and live-online tutoring arms (Ruangguru Privat, Brain Academy offline branches) and a school LMS (Ruangkelas).
- **language_localization**: > Full native Bahasa Indonesia across the entire stack: UI, teacher-presented video lessons, question banks, AI tutor conversation (AiRIS/Roboguru answer in Indonesian), and parent-facing reports delivered in Indonesian via the Parent Center app and WhatsApp. Localization is not a differentiator gap here - it is complete.
- **pricing_model**: > freemium + subscription. Free tier: Roboguru question-answering (app, web, WhatsApp) and AiRIS 'Basic Mode'; paid tier: AiRIS 'Stellar Mode' metered by 'Stellar Energy' credits, and ruangbelajar sold as duration-based subscription packages (3 months, 6 months, 1 year, 1.5 years, 2 years, 2.5 years). ruangbelajar SD is advertised at roughly IDR 300,000-plus per package after a heavy promotional discount; longer durations lower the per-day cost. Private tutoring (Ruangguru Privat) is priced per session/package separately.

**Diagnosis Capability**

- **does_error_type_diagnosis**: > partial - Ruangguru goes beyond a bare right/wrong score, but stops at the TOPIC/CHAPTER level, not at the level of WHY the child got it wrong. Three mechanisms exist: (1) Adapto branches the learning video on the student's answer choice and on a self-reported 'Paham' vs 'Bingung' button, replaying material when the student signals confusion; (2) on a wrong quiz answer the system auto-plays a pembahasan (worked explanation of the correct answer); (3) ruanguji/tryout produces an 'Analisis Pemahaman Materi' listing which topics are mastered vs which need deepening, plus reinforcement recommendations. None of these classify the error into a type (misreading the problem vs conceptual misunderstanding vs arithmetic slip). The output is 'you are weak in Topic X', never 'you subtracted correctly but misread the question' or 'you have a place-value misconception'. No published misconception taxonomy, no distractor-to-misconception mapping, and no analysis of the child's written working.
- **diagnosis_granularity**: > topic-mastery-map. Ruanguji/tryout returns per-topic strength/weakness analysis and reinforcement recommendations; ruangbelajar returns a per-subject report card (rapor) with overall and per-subject grades plus 'which missions/topics to complete next'. This is one step above score-only and one step above trouble-spot-quantity, but well below mcq-distractor-mapped-misconception and far below a full error-type taxonomy.
- **diagnosis_input_modality**: > mcq-distractor (in-video Adapto checkpoint questions, latihan soal, and tryout multiple-choice items drive the adaptive branching and the topic analysis) + self-report (the explicit 'Paham'/'Bingung' button inside Adapto videos is a self-declared comprehension signal, not an inferred one) + free-response-ocr in a limited sense (Roboguru and AiRIS accept a photographed/typed problem, but they read the QUESTION, not the child's handwritten working, so the child's actual error steps are never captured). Handwriting-stroke capture and analysis of the child's own written solution: absent.
- **evidence_quote**: > From ANTARA News (24 March 2022) on the Adapto adaptive feature: 'Di tengah video belajar, guru akan mengecek pemahaman siswa melalui beberapa pertanyaan' ('In the middle of the learning video, the teacher checks the student's understanding through several questions') and, when the student answers incorrectly, 'secara otomatis guru akan memberi pembahasan mengenai jawaban yang benar' ('the teacher automatically gives an explanation of the correct answer'). The remediation is triggered by wrongness alone and delivers the correct-answer walkthrough - there is no branching on WHICH KIND of mistake was made. Separately, Ruangguru's ruanguji page frames its analytics as 'Analisis Pemahaman Materi' that tells the learner 'topik mana yang kamu kuasai dan mana yang perlu pendalaman' ('which topics you have mastered and which need deepening') - topic-level, not error-type-level.

**Parent Role**

- **how_parent_involved**: > gatekeeper + dashboard-only. Gatekeeper: the parent is the paying buyer of the ruangbelajar/Privat subscription and the one who installs the app on the (often shared) phone. Dashboard-only thereafter: Ruangguru ships a dedicated parent application, 'Ruangguru - Parent Center' (Google Play package com.ruangguru.parentapp), letting parents monitor what was covered in each session, read tutor notes, and follow progress for both online and offline branch programs; ruangbelajar additionally produces a monthly Laporan Belajar / 'rapor' with overall and per-subject grades and improvement recommendations, retrievable by texting 'Rapor Anak' to Ruangguru's WhatsApp number. Critically, the parent receives NUMBERS AND TOPIC NAMES, not an actionable explanation of the child's error pattern, and the parent is given no role in the remediation loop - they observe, they do not coach.
- **ai_tutor_directly_to_child**: > yes - unambiguously. AiRIS (personas 'Aurora' and 'Orion') is marketed as Indonesia's first smart interactive AI tutor and is positioned as a 24/7 peer/friend the child talks to directly: learning concepts, doing homework, practice questions, and even off-topic chat about hobbies and future majors. It is available to SD, SMP, and SMA students via the Ruangguru app and ai.ruangguru.com. Roboguru similarly answers the child's photographed/typed questions directly via the app, roboguru.ruangguru.com, and a WhatsApp number, free of charge and across all levels including SD. The AI-to-child channel is therefore fully occupied by the incumbent; it is not a market gap.

**Evidence & Market Signal**

- **key_finding**: > Ruangguru is the maximal incumbent - largest scale in Indonesia (15M+ registered users reported as of June 2025), complete Bahasa Indonesia localization, SD Grade 4 math coverage, a direct-to-child AI tutor (AiRIS), a free AI answer engine (Roboguru), adaptive video (Adapto), and a dedicated parent app - and yet it has NO error-type diagnosis. Its entire diagnostic ceiling is the topic-mastery map: 'which chapter is weak'. Three concrete gaps follow. (1) DIAGNOSIS TYPE: nothing in the stack distinguishes a reading error from a concept error from a calculation error; a child who consistently computes correctly but misreads word problems and a child with a genuine place-value misconception receive the identical output ('weak in Topic X') and the identical remediation (rewatch the topic video). (2) INPUT MODALITY: the platform never sees the child's own working. Adapto reads a multiple-choice tap and a self-reported 'Bingung' button; Roboguru/AiRIS read the QUESTION photo, not the child's handwritten attempt. Self-reported confusion is the weakest possible diagnostic signal for a 9-10 year old, who frequently cannot tell that they misunderstood. (3) PARENT ROLE: the parent is a payer and a passive dashboard viewer receiving grades and topic lists over WhatsApp; they are never handed a diagnosis they can act on. The strategic implication for a Grade 4 math error-type product: do not compete on content library, video quality, brand, or the existence of an AI chat - Ruangguru wins all four decisively. Compete on the diagnostic layer (capturing and classifying the child's actual working into a Reading/Concept/Calculation taxonomy) and on converting the parent from dashboard-observer to informed coach - both of which the incumbent has left structurally empty despite a decade of adaptive-learning investment.
- **evidence_strength**: > moderate - the claim that Ruangguru does NOT do error-type diagnosis rests on convergent negative evidence across the official product pages (ruangbelajar SD, ruanguji, airis, llm-info), the vendor's own LLM-facing information page, and multiple independent Indonesian press reports on Adapto and Roboguru, none of which mention error-type or misconception classification. It is not proof of absence from inside the product (no hands-on subscribed account testing was performed), but the absence is consistent across every primary and secondary source examined.

### Zenius

<a id="zenius"></a>
**Basic Info**

- **name**: Zenius (Zenius Education)
- **category**: competitor_product
- **origin_region**: > Indonesia (founded 2004 in Jakarta by Sabda PS, Wisnu Subekti, and Medy Suharta; began as an offline tutoring center, moved to CD content in 2005, launched zenius.net in 2010, mobile app 2019)
- **language_localization**: > Full native Bahasa Indonesia, and historically Zenius's signature strength was the conversational, first-principles Indonesian explanation style of its tutors (the 'lo-gue' register aimed at teenagers) rather than formal textbook language. ZenBot answers in Indonesian; ZenCore and ZeniusLand are Indonesian-native. Note that the flagship register was designed for teenagers, not for 9-10 year olds, which is a tone mismatch for a Grade 4 audience.

**Diagnosis Capability**

- **does_error_type_diagnosis**: > no (at best partial). Zenius's adaptive layer, ZenCore, explicitly personalizes to the learner's WEAKNESSES - 'Latihan soal yang ada di ZenCore dirancang untuk bisa menyesuaikan pada kelemahan pengguna' - but the unit of weakness is a SKILL or TOPIC within one of three fundamental tracks (mathematics, verbal & logic, English), delivered as a leveled, gamified drill ladder of 135,000+ quiz items up to level 100. The system decides WHAT to drill next; it never states WHY the answer was wrong. There is no misconception taxonomy, no distractor-to-error mapping, and no separation of reading errors from conceptual errors from arithmetic slips. ZenBot, the AI feature, is a photo-to-solution engine: the student uploads a photo of a QUESTION and receives the answer plus a video explanation - it explains the correct path, it does not analyze the student's incorrect path. Pedagogically Zenius has always leaned on 'productive failure' and Assessment for Learning framing, meaning wrong answers are treated as learning opportunities to be explained, not as data to be classified.
- **diagnosis_granularity**: > topic-mastery-map (skill-level adaptive drilling with levels and points). ZenCore adapts item selection to the learner's weak skills and exposes progress through level, points, and ranking; teacher, government, and parent dashboards show 'hasil belajar siswa' - learning outcomes - so that stakeholders can identify where a student needs additional support. That is topic/skill mastery reporting. It does not reach mcq-distractor-mapped-misconception and is far from a full error-type taxonomy.
- **diagnosis_input_modality**: > mcq-distractor (the ZenCore quiz ladder and try-out items are the adaptive signal) + free-response-ocr in a narrow, non-diagnostic sense (ZenBot reads a photographed question via the app or WhatsApp and returns a worked solution with video - it performs OCR on the PROBLEM, not on the child's handwritten attempt, so the child's own working and error steps are never captured or analyzed). Handwriting-stroke capture: absent. Self-report: not a designed mechanism in ZenCore the way Ruangguru's 'Paham/Bingung' button is.
- **evidence_quote**: > From the official ZenCore product page (zencore.id): 'Latihan soal yang ada di ZenCore dirancang untuk bisa menyesuaikan pada kelemahan pengguna' ('The practice questions in ZenCore are designed to adapt to the user's weaknesses'), with the stated purpose of concentrating drilling on those weak areas so learning is thorough and efficient. The object of adaptation is the user's weak SKILL AREA, never the user's TYPE OF ERROR. Complementing this, Zenius's own description of ZenBot is that it 'bisa memberikan jawaban dengan mengunggah foto soal, lengkap dengan video pembahasan' ('can give answers by uploading a photo of the question, complete with explanation videos') - confirming that the AI reads the question and explains the correct solution, and never inspects the student's own wrong working.

**Parent Role**

- **ai_tutor_directly_to_child**: > yes. ZenBot is a student-facing AI assistant reachable 24 hours a day directly by the child through the Zenius application and through WhatsApp: the child photographs a problem and receives an answer plus a video explanation, with no parent in the loop. It is answer-and-explanation oriented rather than conversational-tutor oriented (it is narrower than Ruangguru's AiRIS, which sustains open-ended dialogue and even off-topic chat), but the direct AI-to-child channel unambiguously exists.

**Evidence & Market Signal**

- **key_finding**: > Zenius is simultaneously the strongest pedagogical brand in Indonesian edtech and its clearest cautionary tale, and on the specific gap question it confirms the same void as Ruangguru. Three findings matter. (1) NO ERROR-TYPE DIAGNOSIS: ZenCore, the most sophisticated adaptive engine any Indonesian edtech has shipped (135,000+ items, 100 levels, three fundamental skill tracks, explicitly weakness-adaptive), still diagnoses only WHICH SKILL is weak, never WHY the answer was wrong. ZenBot reads the question, not the child's working. So the two Indonesian market leaders, from opposite design philosophies - Ruangguru's content-and-scale approach and Zenius's first-principles-and-fundamentals approach - independently converged on topic-mastery as the diagnostic ceiling. That convergence is strong evidence the error-type layer is a genuine unoccupied gap rather than something already quietly solved. (2) PARENT ROLE IS THE ONE PLACE ZENIUS WENT FURTHER: ZeniusLand was designed for SD children to use ACCOMPANIED BY a parent, with parent monitoring - the closest any incumbent came to treating the parent as a participant rather than a dashboard viewer. That it existed proves the parent-co-play model is viable in the Indonesian SD market; that it did not save the company proves co-play alone is not a business model. (3) THE BUSINESS-VIABILITY WARNING: Zenius suspended operations in January 2024 after 20 years, with roughly 30 percent of April 2023 revenue consumed by debt service and a failed fundraise in a tight capital market; it returned in July 2024 as a WEB-ONLY platform, explicitly dropping the mobile app as too heavy and expensive to maintain, and only relaunched an app in July 2026. For a product aimed at Grade 4 children on borrowed Android phones, the operational lesson is direct: heavy native-app infrastructure and a broad content library are the cost structures that killed the most beloved brand in the category. A narrow, deep diagnostic wedge with low content-production overhead is the structurally safer entry, and Zenius's retreat has also left the SD segment thinner than it was in 2021.
- **evidence_strength**: > moderate for the product-capability findings, strong for the business-trajectory findings. The 'no error-type diagnosis' conclusion rests on convergent negative evidence: the official ZenCore page (zencore.id, fetched directly) describes adaptation to weaknesses with no mention of error classification, and every secondary description of ZenBot frames it as photo-in/solution-out. No hands-on subscribed-account testing was performed, and zenius.net itself returned HTTP 403 to automated fetching, so live 2026 product surfaces were verified only indirectly. The shutdown, the July 2024 web-only return, and the financial pressure figures are corroborated across Tempo, Detik, Infobank, tek.id, and Wikipedia and are reliable.
- **remediation_efficacy_evidence**: > None found. No RCT, quasi-experiment, or vendor-published efficacy study demonstrating that ZenCore's weakness-adaptive drilling or ZenBot's explanations measurably improve mathematics learning outcomes. Zenius invokes recognized pedagogical frameworks in its marketing - Assessment for Learning and productive failure - but cites them as design rationale, not as evidence that its own implementation works. Combined with the identical absence at Ruangguru, the Indonesian market currently has no incumbent with published proof that its diagnostic layer changes outcomes, which means an entrant that runs even a modest pre/post or quasi-experimental study on error-type-targeted remediation would hold an evidence position no competitor can match.

### Quipper

<a id="quipper"></a>
**Basic Info**

- **name**: Quipper (Quipper School / Quipper School Premium / Quipper Campus; formerly Quipper Video)
- **category**: competitor_product
- **delivery_mode**: > online-app, now predominantly B2B school SaaS. Quipper School / Quipper School Premium is a web-and-mobile LMS sold to schools (teacher portal + student portal), supporting hybrid learning. The direct-to-consumer video subscription (Quipper Video, incl. Quipper Video Masterclass live tutor chat) was shut down in Indonesia on 1 July 2024, so the remaining Indonesian offer reaches children only through their school, not as a standalone parent-purchased app
- **language_localization**: > Full native Bahasa Indonesia — UI, teaching materials, question bank, teacher training modules, blog and customer support are all in Indonesian, produced by a local Jakarta team. Content is locally developed per market rather than translated. Parent-facing Indonesian-language reporting does not exist as a product surface (see how_parent_involved)
- **pricing_model**: > B2B subscription, price not published — the Quipper School Premium page requires a demo booking form: 'Silakan isi formulir Buat Janji Demo untuk mendapatkan penjelasan lengkap seputar paket dan harga'. A free tier of the basic Quipper School LMS has historically been available to teachers. The former B2C consumer subscription (Quipper Video) was discontinued on 1 July 2024 with pro-rata refunds for subscriptions running to 30 June 2025
- **local_curriculum_alignment**: > Strong and explicit. Quipper School Premium states it supports Kurikulum Merdeka, including translation of Capaian Pembelajaran (CP) into Alur Tujuan Pembelajaran (ATP), ready-made Projek Penguatan Profil Pelajar Pancasila (P5) modules, and practice items supporting AKM literacy and numeracy assessment, plus UTBK SNBT university-entrance preparation. This is one of the deepest local-curriculum alignments among the competitor set — but it is aligned to secondary-school curriculum, not primary mathematics

**Diagnosis Capability**

- **does_error_type_diagnosis**: > no. Quipper's diagnosis stops at score and topic level. Mathpresso's B2B/B2G product QANDA Math Coach does advertise a 'comprehensive knowledge graph' for assessing student progress, which is topic-level mastery mapping, still not an error-type taxonomy, and is not exposed in the Indonesian consumer app.
- **diagnosis_input_modality**: > free-response-ocr, but critically the OCR is applied to the QUESTION not to the STUDENT'S ANSWER. This is an inverted use of OCR relative to a diagnostic product: QANDA scans printed/typeset problems to look up or generate a solution. There is no handwriting-stroke capture of the child's working, no MCQ distractor logging, no video-explained reasoning, and no self-report of confusion. A secondary modality exists via the AI chat, where a student can type what they did and be corrected conversationally, but that is unstructured, unlogged as a diagnosis, and depends entirely on the child volunteering their reasoning.
- **ai_tutor_diagnosis_reliability**: > No published validation against any ground-truth error-type labels — Mathpresso publishes benchmark claims for MathGPT's problem-solving accuracy, not for diagnostic accuracy, because diagnosis is not a claimed capability. Reported failure modes are of the solver, not the diagnoser: user and reviewer complaints cite occasional wrong answers, weaker performance on advanced/university-level and out-of-syllabus problems, and bugs. More importantly, the architecture is structurally exposed to the 'correct answer trap' documented in the AI-tutor literature — when a student reaches a right answer via flawed reasoning, systems that evaluate the final answer rather than the reasoning process are blind to the underlying misconception. QANDA is a pure answer-oriented system and therefore sits fully inside that blind spot. Related VLM research (DrawEduMath follow-up) further finds vision-language models underperform specifically on struggling students' work and misdiagnose their errors, which is the exact population and task an error-type diagnosis product must serve.
- **evidence_quote**: > Official App Store feature list for QANDA (Mathpresso Inc.): 'Snap a photo, chat with AI, understand better' — features enumerated are 'Step-by-step explanations', 'Concept quizzes', 'Similar problems', 'Incorrect-answer notebook', 'Formula calculator and QANDA Notes', 'Focus & break timers', 'Study groups with friends', 'AI Friends — Your Study Companions'. No error-type diagnosis, misconception analysis, or parent dashboard appears anywhere in the official listing. Liputan6 (19 Nov 2020) quotes Mathpresso CEO Ray Lee positioning the app on the combination of instant AI search and human tutors: 'Saat ini, belum ada aplikasi edukasi lain yang mampu menggabungkan kedua fitur tersebut dengan sempurna seperti Qanda.'

**Parent Role**

- **ai_tutor_directly_to_child**: > yes — and this is a meaningful market signal. QANDA explicitly supports interactive AI chat with the student: in addition to photographing a question, the child can converse with QANDA AI to work through a problem, and can prompt it for follow-ups such as 'Make a similar question using this concept' to instantly receive a concept quiz. The LLM-based AI Tutor launched in beta in August 2023 and the app now ships 'AI Friends — Your Study Companions'. The entire interaction is child-to-AI with no adult in the loop and no adult visibility into the transcript. So the precedent for AI talking directly to an Indonesian child is already established at scale — the unmet need is not the channel but the diagnosis flowing through it.

**Evidence & Market Signal**

- **key_finding**: > QANDA is proof of enormous Indonesian demand and simultaneously proof that the diagnosis layer is unbuilt. It has reached roughly 2.5 million Indonesian users (Mathpresso claim, Nov 2020, ~10% of smartphone-owning SD-SMA students) and briefly ranked #1-2 in Google Play Education in Indonesia, beating Ruangguru and Zenius — yet it answers questions rather than diagnosing children. Three concrete gaps follow. (1) DIAGNOSIS GAP: OCR is pointed at the printed problem, not at the child's handwritten working, so no error type can be inferred even in principle; the 'incorrect-answer notebook' captures which items were missed, never why. Diagnostic language ('knowledge graph', 'learning assessment') is confined to the B2B QANDA Math Coach and paid QANDA Tutor tiers, absent from the mass-market Indonesian app. (2) PARENT GAP: there is no parent-facing surface at all in the consumer product — no dashboard, no report, no account linking — which for an SD-age child (whose parent is the payer, device owner, and decision-maker) is a structural omission rather than a minor one, and it is exactly why Indonesian coverage treats supervision as a parental chore imposed from outside the app. (3) MISUSE RISK AS OPPORTUNITY: Indonesian academic and journalistic sources converge on the concern that instant-answer AI reduces critical thinking, encourages plagiarism and dependence on instant answers, and makes it hard for teachers to verify authentic work — a failure mode that an error-type-diagnosis product structurally avoids, since diagnosing how a child went wrong requires the child to have actually attempted the work. QANDA is therefore best read as the incumbent that validates the market and defines the gap: massive distribution, real AI-to-child conversation, zero error-type diagnosis, zero parent role.
- **evidence_strength**: > moderate — feature claims and their absences are verified against primary sources (Apple App Store listing, Mathpresso's own product page, Wikipedia), which makes the negative finding on error-type diagnosis and parent dashboards reliable. Scale figures are company-reported press-release numbers with no independent audit, and the Indonesia 2.5M figure is nearly six years old. The one directly on-point academic source (Indonesian SD self-study evaluation) is qualitative and small. Contradicting SEO blog claims about a parent dashboard are weak/anecdotal and were traceable to no primary source.
- **remediation_efficacy_evidence**: > None found. No RCT, quasi-experiment, or controlled study was located showing that QANDA use improves math learning outcomes, and none showing that any diagnostic output of QANDA leads to effective remediation — unsurprising given that QANDA does not claim error-type diagnosis in the first place. Mathpresso publishes engagement and scale metrics (users, searches, speed-to-answer) rather than learning-gain evidence. The available Indonesian scholarship points the other way, raising concerns that instant-answer AI use can depress critical thinking and foster answer-copying dependence, though these are qualitative/argumentative rather than experimental findings. This is itself a competitive opening: the incumbent with 2.5M+ Indonesian users has no efficacy evidence to defend.

### Kumon Indonesia

<a id="kumon-indonesia"></a>
**Basic Info**

- **name**: Kumon Indonesia (Kumon Method, PT Kumon Indonesia)
- **category**: competitor_product
- **origin_region**: > Japan (method created 1954 by Toru Kumon, a high school math teacher, originally as worksheets for his own son). Operated globally in 50+ countries; Kumon Indonesia runs as a franchised centre network with a localized Indonesian-language site and area-tiered pricing.
- **target_age**: > Prasekolah (preschool, from roughly age 3) through SMA (high school). Pricing is explicitly tiered as 'Prasekolah-SD' and 'SMP-SMA', confirming elementary is a first-class segment. Notably, students are commonly started 1-3 grade levels BELOW their school grade to guarantee early perfect scores and build foundations — so an SD child's Kumon level is deliberately decoupled from their school grade.
- **delivery_mode**: > offline-tutoring, now hybrid. The core is a physical centre (kelas Kumon) attended typically twice a week, with paper worksheets done daily at home; a human instructor (pembimbing) grades and observes. KUMON CONNECT adds a tablet/web layer for digital worksheets, instructor comments and a parent-visible study record, making the model hybrid rather than purely offline. Kumon Connect currently covers Math levels 6A-O.
- **language_localization**: > Full Indonesian localization — official site id.kumonglobal.com operates in Bahasa Indonesia, worksheets and instruction are delivered in Indonesian, parent orientation (orientasi orang tua) and learning consultation (konsultasi belajar) are conducted face-to-face in Indonesian by the local instructor, and Kumon Indonesia even offers Bahasa Indonesia as a subject alongside Matematika and Bahasa Inggris. This is the deepest local-language parent communication of any competitor in this set — but it is delivered by a human, not by the product.
- **pricing_model**: > subscription — monthly fee per subject per student, plus a one-time registration fee, tiered across five geographic areas (effective 1 January 2026). Registration: Rp350,000 (Areas 1-4) / Rp300,000 (Area 5). Monthly Prasekolah-SD: Area 1 (Jabodetabek & Karawang) Rp525,000; Area 2 Rp490,000; Area 3 (Bali, most of West Java, Kalimantan, Sulawesi, Sumatera, Greater Surabaya) Rp480,000; Area 4 Rp440,000; Area 5 (Aceh, Bengkulu, Yogyakarta, Jambi, Central Java, Lampung, NTB, West Sumatera) Rp430,000. SMP-SMA runs Rp480,000-Rp575,000. Costs multiply per subject — an SD student taking Math + English + Indonesian in Jabodetabek was reported at ~Rp1,925,000 for registration plus first month. Free placement test (tes penempatan), free consultation and short free-trial periods are used as acquisition offers.
- **local_curriculum_alignment**: > No — and this is deliberate, not an oversight. Kumon runs an independent, globally standardized curriculum designed to supplement rather than mirror school learning; the same worksheets are used in every Kumon class worldwide. It is not mapped to Kurikulum Merdeka or K13, and the practice of starting students below their school grade means Kumon level and school grade intentionally diverge. Consequence for the gap analysis: a Kumon parent gets a progress signal on Kumon's own ladder that does not tell them how their child is doing against the Indonesian national curriculum their child is actually assessed on at school.

**Diagnosis Capability**

- **does_error_type_diagnosis**: > no — placement and evaluation are level/topic-based and speed-and-accuracy-based, not error-type-based. The placement test (tes penempatan) determines a STARTING POINT on Kumon's level ladder; per Kumon Indonesia's own FAQ the tests simply 'cover a range of basic arithmetic and reading comprehension exercises', with no disclosed error-type classification. Ongoing evaluation runs on two explicit criteria — accuracy (score) and Standard Completion Time (SCT), an internationally derived per-worksheet-set time benchmark. Mastery is defined as completing a packet with good accuracy WITHIN the SCT; failing either triggers repetition of the same set. That is a repeat-or-advance decision rule, not a diagnosis of what kind of mistake was made. PARTIAL COUNTERWEIGHT: an experienced human instructor does perform informal, undocumented error analysis — Kumon states that close observation of worksheet study and analysis of completed worksheets lets instructors judge whether repetition will help, and that when errors appear instructors deliberately withhold the answer and instead give hints so the student realises on their own 'what parts are incorrect and the reasons why'. So error-type reasoning exists in the instructor's head, but it is tacit, unstandardized, instructor-quality-dependent, never captured as structured data, and never surfaced to the parent as a named error type.
- **diagnosis_granularity**: > topic-mastery-map (level placement on the Kumon ladder) combined with trouble-spot-quantity (error counts per worksheet and completion time versus SCT, visible over time in Kumon Connect). It does not reach mcq-distractor-mapped-misconception or full-error-type-taxonomy. The signal a parent receives is 'how many wrong, how fast, which level' — never 'misread the question' versus 'wrong concept' versus 'arithmetic slip'.
- **diagnosis_input_modality**: > free-response handwritten worksheet, human-graded. The child writes full working by hand on paper worksheets; the instructor or grader marks them and observes the student working in the centre. Under KUMON CONNECT the same worksheets are completed on a tablet, with instructors and graders able to write comments or underline material directly on the worksheet and send short messages including 'guidance on common mistakes'. This is the richest raw input of any competitor in the set — actual handwritten student working is physically available — yet it is consumed by human eyes and then discarded rather than converted into structured error-type data. Not MCQ-distractor, not OCR, not video-explained, not self-report.
- **ai_tutor_diagnosis_reliability**: > Not applicable in the AI sense — there is no AI diagnostic engine; judgement is human (pembimbing) and therefore varies with instructor experience and centre quality, with no published inter-rater reliability or validation against ground-truth error labels. The systematic evaluation that does exist is the SCT benchmark, which is statistically derived from international student data and is reliable for what it measures (speed and accuracy) but is by construction blind to error TYPE. Known failure mode of the approach as a whole, widely reported by parents and reviewers: the speed-and-accuracy optimization can produce children who calculate fast but cannot handle word problems or explain their reasoning — i.e. precisely the conceptual and reading-comprehension error types the evaluation scheme never measures. Critics also cite over-reliance on rote repetition limiting conceptual understanding, thin coverage of high-level word problems, and repetition-driven boredom or burnout.
- **evidence_quote**: > Kumon Indonesia, official method page: 'Melalui tes penempatan, rencana belajar dibuat secara individu untuk memastikan bahwa setiap siswa diberikan tantangan saat belajar menggunakan lembar kerja Kumon yang disesuaikan dengan tingkatan yang tepat untuk mereka.' — the placement test's stated output is an individual study plan at the right LEVEL, not an error profile. Kumon Indonesia FAQ on the proficiency tests: 'Each test covers a range of basic arithmetic and reading comprehension exercises in English and/or native language' — no error-type dimension disclosed. On mastery: 'Based on international statistics derived from students, the Standard Completion Time (SCT) has been determined for each set of worksheets. Instructors use the SCT as a guideline and compare it with a student's completion time' — mastery is 'defined by two unambiguous criteria - speed and accuracy'. On error handling: 'Close observation of the student's worksheet study and analysis of their completed worksheets allow instructors to determine whether repetition will result in smoother study for a student. If there are any errors on the worksheets, instructors avoid giving the students the answers right away. Instead, they give students hints so that they realise on their own what parts are incorrect and the reasons why.' — the reasons why are surfaced to the child verbally by a human, never recorded or classified. On the instructor's role: 'memberikan petunjuk sampai siswa tersebut bisa maju dengan percaya diri tanpa memerlukan bantuan.'

**Parent Role**

- **ai_tutor_directly_to_child**: > no — there is no AI tutor and no conversational agent of any kind. All explanation reaching the child comes from (a) the worked examples printed at the top of each worksheet, which are designed so the child can self-learn without being taught, and (b) hints given verbally by the human pembimbing during centre sessions. Kumon Connect adds only asynchronous instructor comments, underlines and short text messages on worksheets — human-authored, not generated. This is the sharpest structural contrast with QANDA, which does chat directly with the child: Kumon has the human trust and the parent relationship but no scalable per-child explanation channel; QANDA has the direct-to-child AI channel but no parent relationship and no diagnosis. Neither has both.

**Evidence & Market Signal**

- **key_finding**: > Kumon is the trust and parent-relationship benchmark, and simultaneously the clearest demonstration that error-TYPE diagnosis is missing even in the market's most established, most expensive, most parent-integrated math offering. Four points matter for the gap analysis. (1) EVALUATION IS LEVEL-AND-SPEED, NOT ERROR-TYPE: placement sets a starting level; ongoing mastery is judged purely on accuracy plus Standard Completion Time, producing a binary repeat-or-advance decision. The child's error type is never classified, named, tracked, or reported. (2) THE RICHEST POSSIBLE DIAGNOSTIC INPUT IS BEING THROWN AWAY: Kumon physically collects handwritten step-by-step working from millions of children daily — the exact substrate needed to distinguish misreading from concept failure from arithmetic slip — but consumes it through a human grader's eyes and reduces it to a score and a time. Tacit instructor insight exists but is unstandardized, instructor-dependent, and never captured as data. (3) PARENT REPORTING IS REAL BUT GRANULARITY-LIMITED: via monthly orientation, free consultation, and Kumon Connect's Study Records, Indonesian parents already receive scores, completion times, error counts trending over time, and instructor commentary in Bahasa Indonesia. This proves Indonesian parents will pay Rp430,000-575,000 per subject per month and show up monthly for a credible progress narrative — demand for parent reporting is established, not speculative. What they receive is 'how many wrong and how fast', never 'what kind of wrong'. (4) THE KNOWN FAILURE MODE MAPS EXACTLY ONTO THE GAP: the most persistent criticism of Kumon — children who compute fast but cannot do word problems or explain their reasoning — is the predictable outcome of an evaluation scheme that measures speed and accuracy while remaining blind to reading and conceptual error types. Positioning implication: a product that diagnoses error type from the child's own working, reports it to the parent in plain Indonesian, and is mapped to Kurikulum Merdeka would address Kumon's blind spot on all three axes (error type, curriculum alignment, and cost) while borrowing its proven parent-reporting demand.
- **evidence_strength**: > strong for pricing, delivery model, placement mechanics, SCT-based mastery criteria and parent-facing features — all verified against Kumon's own official Indonesian and global pages (id.kumonglobal.com fee schedule, id.kumonasiaoceania.com method and FAQ pages, kumon.com Kumon Connect and instructor-role pages). Moderate for the criticism of conceptual/word-problem weakness, which is consistent across many independent reviews and parent reports but is largely anecdotal and comparison-blog sourced rather than experimental. Weak/uncertain for current Indonesia-specific student and centre counts, which rest on a dated academic figure.
- **remediation_efficacy_evidence**: > Some experimental evidence exists for the METHOD as a whole, but none for error-type diagnosis (which the method does not perform). The strongest is a randomized field experiment in Bangladesh ('Individualized Self-learning Program to Improve Primary Education' / 'Fighting the Learning Crisis in Developing Countries: A Randomized Experiment of Self-Learning at the Right Level', published in Economic Development and Cultural Change) in which primary students received 30-minute Kumon sessions before regular lessons; it reported gains in cognitive ability measured by math test scores and national public examinations, plus non-cognitive gains in self-esteem and grit, and pioneered time-reduction and time-adjusted score measures. A longitudinal study in Pakistan (Global Social Sciences Review) found the Kumon method more effective than traditional lecture instruction for Grade 6 mathematics, with equal benefit for boys and girls. Important caveats for this analysis: these test 'teaching at the right level plus daily repetition', which is the level-placement mechanism, NOT diagnosis of error types; no study isolates a diagnostic component; no Indonesia-specific RCT was found; and the Bangladesh trial was run in a developing-country primary context with a low-performing counterfactual, so effect sizes may not transfer to Indonesian urban SD students. Net: the level-placement-plus-repetition engine has real supporting evidence, while the error-type diagnosis question remains entirely untested because no incumbent implements it.

<a id="ixl"></a>
**Basic Info**

- **name**: IXL (IXL Learning)
- **category**: competitor_product
- **origin_region**: > Global, US-origin (IXL Learning, San Mateo, California). Operates localized country sites for the US, UK, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, India, Singapore and South Africa. **Diagnosis Capability**

- **does_error_type_diagnosis**: > no (at best partial, and not in the sense that matters). This confirms and sharpens the earlier finding. IXL has two things that look like diagnosis and are not: (1) Trouble Spots, which is a QUANTITY TRIGGER - a trouble spot fires when a student misses three questions within a specific item type inside a skill. It tells you WHICH item type the child keeps missing and lets an adult scroll the actual missed questions, but it never says WHY: no label distinguishing a misread question from a conceptual misconception from an arithmetic slip. The adult must read the missed questions and infer the cause themselves. (2) The Real-Time Diagnostic, which is a LEVEL ESTIMATOR - it re-calibrates difficulty and reports a working grade level per strand. It is psychometric placement, not error analysis. Nothing in IXL's official diagnostic documentation, its FAQ blog post, or its teacher guides mentions misconceptions or error types at all.
- **diagnosis_granularity**: > A combination of topic-mastery-map and trouble-spot-quantity - explicitly NOT mcq-distractor-mapped-misconception and not a full error-type taxonomy. Concretely: (a) SmartScore per skill, 0-100, mastery-style, which rises and falls with correctness, question difficulty, recency and consistency; (b) diagnostic levels on a 0-800+ scale where the number encodes working grade level - a level of 400 means readiness to start 4th-grade skills and 450 means about halfway through the 4th-grade curriculum, with preschool at 0-50 and kindergarten at 50-100; levels are reported per strand via the Diagnostic Strand Analysis; (c) Trouble Spots at item-type granularity, triggered at three misses. What IS validated is the platform's overall achievement effect (ESSA Tier 1 and Tier 2 studies, below) and, indirectly, the adaptive engine. Known failure modes relevant to us: (1) the answer-only channel means the 'correct answer trap' is structural - a lucky guess raises SmartScore identically to genuine understanding; (2) SmartScore is widely criticised by students and parents as punitive, since a wrong answer near the top of a skill drops the score sharply and can wipe out substantial progress, which drives frustration, guessing and answer-seeking behaviour (an entire cottage industry of third-party IXL answer solvers and AI helpers exists precisely because of this dynamic) - and guessing further corrupts whatever diagnostic signal the answer stream carries; (3) Trouble Spots' three-miss trigger is a threshold heuristic with no published validation that three misses indicates a real, stable, remediable difficulty rather than fatigue or inattention.

On the Diagnostic, from IXL's official FAQ: 'A student's diagnostic level represents their working grade level. So a level of 400 represents a readiness to start working on 4th grade skills' - and the same FAQ makes no mention whatsoever of misconceptions or error types. On what parents get: 'IXL automatically creates a personalized learning plan of the exact skills your child is ready to learn next.' **Evidence & Market Signal**

- **key_finding**: > Verified: IXL's 'diagnostic' is difficulty re-calibration, not error-type classification. The Real-Time Diagnostic answers 'what grade level is this child working at, per strand?' in about 45 minutes per subject, kept current by 10-15 questions a week; Trouble Spots answers 'which item type has this child missed three or more times?'. Neither answers 'is this a reading error, a conceptual misconception, or a calculation slip?' - and IXL's own documentation never uses that vocabulary. The structural cause is the input channel: IXL only observes final answers (MCQ, typed values, drag-and-drop), so the child's reasoning process is never captured and therefore cannot be classified. This is the market gap in its clearest form. IXL is the most sophisticated, best-evidenced adaptive practice platform in the world for primary math (ESSA Tier 1 RCT), it has been at this for two decades, it has the largest response dataset in the category, and it still stops at 'which skill' rather than 'which kind of mistake' - and it still hands the parent a number rather than an explanation. Add the Indonesian-market barriers (English-only, roughly IDR 150,000-320,000/month, no Kurikulum Merdeka alignment, no offline design, no AI tutor speaking to the child) and IXL is not a defensible incumbent in this segment on either the diagnosis axis or the parent axis.
- **evidence_strength**: > strong for the negative finding, strong-but-off-target for efficacy. The claim that IXL does not classify error types is well supported: it rests on IXL's own official teacher guides, its Diagnostic FAQ, its user guides and its product pages, in all of which the vocabulary is uniformly skills, levels and item types, with misconception and error type entirely absent - and it is corroborated by the mechanism (three-miss threshold; answer-only input). The efficacy evidence is genuinely strong by edtech standards (ESSA Tier 1 RCT plus Tier 2 quasi-experiment) but is vendor-commissioned and measures the whole product, so it cannot be read as evidence for the diagnostic layer specifically.
- **remediation_efficacy_evidence**: > Yes for the platform, no for the diagnosis mechanism - an important distinction for our gap thesis. IXL can point to the strongest evidence base in this competitor set: a Johns Hopkins RCT meeting ESSA Tier 1 and WWC standards (+10 points on Star Math, 13-17 points for several underserved subgroups) and an SRI-reviewed three-year quasi-experimental study meeting ESSA Tier 2. But every one of these tests the bundle - adaptive sequencing, spaced practice, volume of items, teacher assignment, step-by-step explanations - not the isolated contribution of identifying what kind of mistake a child makes. Since IXL never produces an error-type diagnosis, no study can show that such a diagnosis improves outcomes. The claim 'diagnosing error type improves learning' therefore remains empirically open in the commercial market, which is simultaneously the opportunity and the risk for a product built on that premise.

### Photomath

<a id="photomath"></a>
**Basic Info**

- **name**: Photomath (Photomath Inc., owned by Google)
- **category**: competitor_product
- **origin_region**: > Croatia-origin, global reach. Founded in Zagreb by Damir Sabol (spun out of Microblink's OCR technology, launched 2014). It is not designed for a 9-11 year old: there is no age-appropriate UI, no child onboarding, no reading-level adaptation, and the whole interaction assumes a learner who can already read a printed math problem and judge whether the returned solution is the one they needed. - **local_curriculum_alignment**: > None. Photomath is deliberately curriculum-agnostic: it solves whatever equation the camera sees, independent of syllabus, sequence or grade. There is no Kurikulum Merdeka or K13 mapping, no CP/ATP alignment, no Indonesian textbook coverage in the Textbook Solutions library (that library is dominated by US titles), and no notion of 'what a kelas 4 child should be able to do this semester'. Because it has no curriculum model, it also has no concept of prerequisite gaps - it cannot say 'this child failed this because a kelas 3 skill is missing'.

**Diagnosis Capability**

- **does_error_type_diagnosis**: > no. This is the clearest 'no' in the entire competitor set, and it is structural rather than a missing feature. Photomath's input is a photograph of the PROBLEM, not of the CHILD'S WORK. It OCRs the question, runs a symbolic solver, and renders a canonical solution path. At no point does it ingest, model, or evaluate what the student did. There is no answer submitted by the student, no score, no mastery model, no error label, no misconception vocabulary anywhere in the product or its documentation. Reviews that describe it as a way to 'find where you went wrong' are describing a MANUAL human act: the student (or parent) reads Photomath's steps, compares them line by line against their own working, and infers the discrepancy themselves. The app performs no part of that comparison. Photomath answers 'how is this problem solved?'; it never answers 'what did you get wrong, and what kind of wrong was it?'
- **diagnosis_granularity**: > None - it sits below 'score-only' on the stated scale. Score-only implies at least an assessment of the learner; Photomath produces no assessment of the learner at all, only an artifact about the problem. There is no per-topic mastery map, no trouble-spot counter, no distractor-mapped misconception, and obviously no B/K/H-style error-type taxonomy. The only granularity it offers is granularity of the SOLUTION: the ability to expand a step, see it broken into sub-steps, view an animated tutorial, or request an alternative solution method (e.g. factoring vs quadratic formula). That is explanatory granularity, not diagnostic granularity - a distinction worth stating explicitly in the gap analysis, because 'step-by-step' is routinely mis-sold as if it were diagnosis.
- **diagnosis_input_modality**: > free-response-ocr, but of the wrong artifact. The camera + OCR pipeline (inherited from Microblink, historically the best-in-class in this category) reads printed and handwritten problem statements. It does NOT capture handwriting strokes as a reasoning trace, does not accept the student's attempted answer for grading, has no MCQ distractors, no video-explained reasoning and no self-report of strategy. Because the student's own process never enters the system, error-type classification is impossible in principle, not just unimplemented. Known input-side failure modes: OCR misreads of messy child handwriting, and confusion on multi-part or context-heavy word problems where the app may solve a different problem than the one intended.
- **ai_tutor_diagnosis_reliability**: > There is no diagnosis to validate, so the honest answer is absence rather than unreliability. What can be said about correctness: the symbolic solver is generally accurate on well-posed, cleanly captured symbolic problems - this is its historical strength versus general-purpose LLM math chat - but it degrades on word problems, geometry with diagrams, ambiguous notation and poor captures, and it may silently solve a misread version of the problem. The relevant failure modes for our thesis are pedagogical rather than computational: (1) the 'correct-solution trap' - the child sees a correct path and mistakes recognition for understanding, the well-documented 'academic theater' effect where steps are copied without engagement; (2) no discrimination between a child who was one sign-error away and a child who had no idea, since both receive the identical canonical walkthrough; (3) it is a first-class cheating vector, which is why teacher-facing advice and parent guides frame it defensively (the '15-minute rule': attempt first, scan last). Independent parent guidance explicitly contrasts it with Khanmigo, which withholds answers and asks leading questions, as the 'cheat-proof' alternative.
- **evidence_quote**: > From Photomath's own product page, the entire value proposition is expressed in solution vocabulary, never assessment vocabulary: 'step-by-step explanations', 'custom visual aids', extra 'how and why tips', and multiple solution methods - with no mention of misconceptions, error types, mastery or student assessment anywhere. Third-party reviews confirm the diagnosis is manual and human, not automated: students 'can compare their steps with the steps provided by the app and identify where they went wrong' - i.e. the comparison is performed by the student, not by Photomath. An independent parent's guide reviewing this class of app notes that Khanmigo is 'the only one that is 100% cheat-proof' because 'it won't give the answer; it will only ask leading questions', implicitly classifying Photomath as an answer-giver rather than a diagnostic tutor.

**Parent Role**

- **how_parent_involved**: > none, in any designed sense - and this is the sharpest contrast with our product thesis. Photomath has no parent account, no child account linked to a parent, no dashboard, no progress report, no notification, no weekly summary and no parental controls. There is not even a login model that distinguishes a parent from a child. The only 'parent role' that exists is one the market invented on its own: parents downloading the app for themselves and using it to check or explain their child's homework when they cannot remember the maths. That is the parent as a self-serve solver user, not as a supported coach. Independent parent guidance therefore has to substitute manual strategies for missing product features - 'sit with your kid during one homework session' to observe usage - precisely because the product surfaces nothing to the parent. Net: parents are neither gatekeeper nor dashboard-viewer; they are unmodelled.

**Evidence & Market Signal**

- **key_finding**: > Photomath is the purest available demonstration that 'AI step-by-step solver' and 'diagnosis' are orthogonal, not adjacent. With 100M+ downloads, best-in-class OCR, Google's resources behind it, and 32 languages including Bahasa Indonesia, it still produces exactly zero information about the learner. It photographs the PROBLEM, not the CHILD'S WORK - so there is no student response in the system to classify, and the B/K/H question (was this a reading error, a concept error, or a calculation error?) is not merely unanswered but unanswerable within its architecture. Two consequences matter for the gap thesis. First, on the diagnosis axis it is not a competitor at all; it is a reference implementation of the thing we are differentiating from, and its enormous adoption proves that market demand for 'show me the answer' is already saturated while demand for 'tell me what my child misunderstands' is untouched. Second, on the parent axis it is a total void - no parent account, no report, no notification - and yet a substantial share of its real usage is parents checking homework, which is direct evidence of unmet parental demand being served by an unsuitable tool. The one genuine competitive threat it poses is distribution and price: it is free, already installed, already in Indonesian, and it makes the homework 'problem' appear solved to a parent who has not yet realized that a solved problem and a learning child are different things. Also relevant: the strongest counter-positioning already exists elsewhere in the market (Khanmigo's answer-withholding Socratic design), which parent guides now recommend over Photomath - a signal that the market is starting to want what Photomath structurally cannot offer.
- **evidence_strength**: > strong for the negative findings, weak-to-absent for any learning claim. The claims 'does not diagnose error types', 'has no parent dashboard' and 'has no AI tutor chat' are strongly supported because they rest on the primary source itself: Photomath's own site, help centre and app-store listings describe only solving, steps, animated tutorials and textbook solutions, and contain no assessment, misconception, error-type, parent-account or chat vocabulary at all - corroborated by multiple independent 2026 reviews and by a parent-oriented guide. Absence-of-feature evidence from primary product documentation is about as reliable as this category gets. Conversely there is essentially no evidence of any kind that Photomath improves learning outcomes; that claim is not made and not tested. Confidence is lowest on the 2026 Gemini/chat question, where Google's roadmap is opaque.
- **remediation_efficacy_evidence**: > None. There is no RCT, no quasi-experimental study, no ESSA tier, no third-party efficacy certification and no vendor-commissioned impact study for Photomath - and there could not be one for its diagnosis, since it produces no diagnosis. The available academic literature touching Photomath is dominated by small descriptive studies (frequently Indonesian, on perception, interest and motivation) and by a critical strand in the mathematics-education literature warning about answer-copying, dependency and the illusion of understanding created by step-by-step solvers. This is the mirror image of Eedi's position in this research set: Photomath has the distribution but no evidence and no diagnosis; Eedi has the diagnosis and the evidence but not the distribution in this market.

### Eedi

<a id="eedi"></a>
**Basic Info**

- **name**: Eedi (Eedi Ltd / Eedi Labs, formerly Diagnostic Questions)
- **category**: diagnostic_benchmark
- **origin_region**: > United Kingdom (London). Grew out of Craig Barton's Diagnostic Questions project and a decade of UK mathematics-misconception research. Claims usage in 56 countries and 'over 160,000 teachers in 19,000 schools', but the installed base is overwhelmingly UK schools; the active expansion front is the United States (Eedi Labs' constrained AI Tutor RCT with Accelerate in US middle schools, plus a US pricing page and Common Core-facing product). Stated future expansion targets are the US, India, Africa and Latin America - Southeast Asia is not named.
- **target_age**: > Roughly 9-14 years, covering Year 5 through Year 9 in England and Wales (upper KS2 through KS3), with the question library extending across K-12 mathematics and the GCSE-facing framing at the top end. This is the single most important structural fact for our purposes: Eedi's lower bound (Year 5-6, ages 9-11) DOES overlap the Indonesian SD kelas 4-6 band, so it is a genuine reference design for diagnostic assessment at upper-primary level - but its centre of gravity is KS3 secondary, its RCT evidence is drawn almost entirely from ages 11-15, and its content design assumes a school and a teacher, not a 10-year-old at home on a borrowed phone.
- **delivery_mode**: > online-app, school-mediated. The primary product is a teacher-assigned homework and classroom platform: teacher builds/assigns a quiz of diagnostic questions, students answer, the platform reports misconceptions at individual and class level, and a 'Fix' page surfaces unresolved misconceptions for reteaching. A direct-to-family tier exists (family.eedi.com signup, parent accounts, an Eedi Plus consumer offer) but it is secondary to the school channel. Eedi Labs additionally runs a tutoring layer that is hybrid by design: a constrained AI tutor operating under live human tutor supervision, where a qualified tutor reviews and approves every AI message before it reaches the student. No offline/centre-based delivery.
- **local_curriculum_alignment**: > None for Indonesia. Eedi aligns to the English National Curriculum and GCSE, with explicit White Rose Maths scheme-of-work mapping (the WhatWorked pilot was run on a White Rose KS3 topic), and to US standards/Common Core for its American product. There is no Kurikulum Merdeka or K13 mapping, no CP/ATP structure, and the topic sequencing follows UK progression. Note that misconceptions themselves are substantially universal (place-value errors, fraction-as-two-integers, misapplied 'multiplication makes bigger'), so the RESEARCH transfers even though the PRODUCT does not - which is precisely why Eedi is the right benchmark rather than the right competitor.

**Diagnosis Capability**

- **does_error_type_diagnosis**: > yes - and this is the only unambiguous 'yes' in the whole research set. Eedi's product is diagnosis; the practice content is downstream of it. Every question is a purpose-built diagnostic question with one correct answer and exactly three distractors, where each distractor is authored to be the answer a student arrives at by holding one specific, named, commonly-occurring misconception. When a child selects option C, the platform does not merely record 'incorrect' - it asserts a named misconception, drawn from a misconception taxonomy built over roughly a decade of research and calibrated against historical response data from thousands of prior students on the same item. Teachers see individual and class-level misconception data, with each incorrect option annotated by the misconception it indicates, and post-assignment results are classified into three states: correct, misconception resolved (after intervention), and misconception unresolved. That third state is an error-type-level construct, not a score.
- **diagnosis_granularity**: > mcq-distractor-mapped-misconception - the canonical example of that category, and one notch below a full error-type taxonomy in one specific and important respect. What it delivers: a named misconception per wrong answer, with pre-assignment prediction of which misconceptions are likely (from historical data on that item), per-student and per-class aggregation, and a resolved/unresolved lifecycle. What it does NOT deliver, and this matters directly for the B/K/H design: the taxonomy is a taxonomy of CONCEPTUAL misconceptions (the 'K' axis), organised per item and per topic, and it does not cleanly separate reading/comprehension failure from conceptual misunderstanding from calculation slip. A child who misread the question, and a child who understood the question but holds the misconception the distractor encodes, produce the identical click and receive the identical label. Eedi's own help documentation is explicit that the system works at question level from answer selection, and acknowledges no limitations - it does not address guessing, nor a correct answer reached by faulty reasoning. So: far deeper than any competitor product in this set, but still a single-axis (concept) rather than a three-axis (Baca/Konsep/Hitung) diagnosis.
- **diagnosis_input_modality**: > mcq-distractor, essentially exclusively. Diagnosis is inferred from which of four options the student clicks; nothing else is required, and Craig Barton's design philosophy is explicitly that a well-constructed diagnostic question 'should work on its own' without further probing. Asking students to explain their reasoning IS part of the surrounding pedagogy - Barton recommends explanation prompts, exit tickets with an explanation field, and mini-whiteboard reasoning, specifically because they help when the teacher 'suspects some students may be guessing' or when the correct answer can be reached by different routes - but that is teacher-side classroom practice layered on top, not the platform's diagnostic input. There is no free-response OCR, no photograph of the child's working, no handwriting-stroke capture and no video-explained reasoning. The Eedi Labs AI tutor adds a real chat channel with the student, which is a genuine second modality and the direction the diagnosis is evolving, but the underlying misconception identification still originates from the distractor choice.
- **ai_tutor_diagnosis_reliability**: > Partially validated, with two identified failure modes. On the positive side, the distractor-to-misconception mapping is not merely authored assertion: it is calibrated against a very large longitudinal response corpus (the same corpus released for the NeurIPS 2020 Education Challenge on the Eedi/Diagnostic Questions dataset), and teachers are shown historical distributions of which misconceptions previous cohorts exhibited on the same item - so the mapping has empirical support in aggregate. Eedi Labs also published an RCT-grade safety audit of the AI layer: across 3,617 AI-generated tutoring messages, human tutors approved 76.4% without edits, and the audit found zero instances of harmful or risky content and only five factual errors (0.1%). Failure modes: (1) the CORRECT ANSWER TRAP is structurally unaddressed - a guess, a correct answer reached by faulty reasoning, and genuine mastery are indistinguishable to a 4-option MCQ, and Eedi's own misconception documentation does not acknowledge this; the mitigation lives in teacher practice (ask for explanations) rather than in the product. (2) Attribution ambiguity - selecting a distractor is evidence for the mapped misconception but not proof; misreading, mis-clicking, careless arithmetic or a different-but-unlisted misconception all collapse into the same signal, so an individual diagnosis is probabilistic even though the platform presents it as categorical. For our purposes both failure modes are the argument for capturing the child's working rather than only their final selection.
- **evidence_quote**: > On the mechanism: 'A diagnostic question has one correct answer, three incorrect answers, and each incorrect answer reveals a specific mistake or misconception' (Craig Barton, Eedi). On scale and structure: the core of the platform is 'a vast library of over 60,000 diagnostic questions' in which 'every question incorporates one correct answer and three incorrect answers (distractors), with each distractor mapped to a specific, common student misconception', so that 'when a student answers a diagnostic question incorrectly, this structure allows the platform to precisely identify the underlying error in their thinking based solely on their answer choice.' On the reporting lifecycle, Eedi's help centre classifies post-assignment results as correct, misconception resolved, and misconception unresolved, and provides a 'Fix' page of questions with unresolved misconceptions. On the marketing claim under scrutiny: Eedi is described as 'proven by a large-scale randomized controlled trial to double the rate of learning in math.' On the AI tutor's dependence on that diagnosis: the model 'received rich contextual information including question text, student responses, and identified misconceptions.'

**Parent Role**

- **how_parent_involved**: > dashboard-only, and deliberately de-skilled - the most interesting parent design in this research set, and instructive precisely because of what it chooses NOT to do. Parents get an Eedi for Parents account linked to their child, showing homework assignments, quiz results, progress charts by topic and areas to improve, plus 'personalised daily actions'. Crucially, Eedi's own product writing states that parent notifications deliberately do NOT focus on in-depth mathematical concepts; instead they nudge the parent toward building a supportive home learning environment, explicitly so that involvement does 'not require parents to be an expert in maths or spend hours on homework'. In other words: Eedi HAS the misconception-level diagnosis, and consciously withholds most of it from the parent, translating it into low-cognitive-load behavioural prompts instead. That is a validated design precedent for the low-numeracy-parent problem - but it is also, arguably, a missed opportunity, and it is the exact seam our product targets: telling the parent WHAT KIND of mistake the child made, in language they can act on, rather than either dumping the misconception taxonomy on them or reducing them to a cheerleader. The parent is not a gatekeeper in the school channel (the teacher assigns the work); in the family channel the parent signs up and adds the child, so there the parent is gatekeeper plus dashboard-viewer.

**Evidence & Market Signal**

- **key_finding**: > Eedi is the closest existing thing to the B/K/H thesis, it works, it has real evidence - and it is absent from this market, aimed at the wrong end of the age range, and deliberately withholds its diagnosis from parents. Four findings matter. (1) GRANULARITY IS REAL BUT SINGLE-AXIS: 60,000+ MCQs with every distractor mapped to a named misconception is genuine error-type diagnosis, not dressed-up scoring - it validates the core premise that identifying the KIND of mistake is a viable, buildable product. But the axis is conceptual misconception only; misreading and calculation slips are not separated from concept errors, because a click carries no trace of process. That is a specific, defensible differentiation for a three-axis B/K/H design and it points to the required mechanism: capture the child's working, not just their selection. (2) SOUTHEAST ASIA IS OPEN: no evidence of any Indonesian or SEA market entry, no Bahasa Indonesia, no Kurikulum Merdeka mapping; stated expansion targets are the US, India, Africa and Latin America. Its lower age band (Year 5-6) overlaps SD kelas 4-6, so the design transfers, but the product does not. (3) THE RCT CLAIM NEEDS CAREFUL HANDLING: the headline 'doubles the rate of learning' is a marketing claim, not a finding from the EEF trial - the EEF efficacy trial (28,930 students, 158 schools, Dec 2017-Jun 2021, evaluated by AlphaPlus and Manchester Metropolitan) collected NO primary outcome because of Covid-19 and reports NO impact on maths attainment. The defensible evidence is elsewhere and is more modest but genuinely good by edtech standards: a WhatWorked Education RCT with an ACTIVE control (2,901 Year 7 students, 20 UK schools, 2023-24, compared against Sparx and MathsWatch rather than against nothing) finding 2 months of additional progress overall, 4 months for students using it 10-15 minutes weekly, 3 months for below-median and Pupil Premium students; plus an EduEvidence gold efficacy certificate from a two-year RCT reaching d=0.46 at 18 months and d=0.30 at 24 months. (4) THE PARENT CHANNEL IS DELIBERATELY THIN: Eedi decided its parents should get behavioural nudges rather than misconception detail, on the explicit grounds that parents are not maths experts and have no time. That is a real, evidence-informed design constraint we must respect - and simultaneously the clearest gap: nobody has yet tried to give a low-numeracy parent an ACTIONABLE, plain-language error-type diagnosis rather than either a number or a nudge.
- **evidence_strength**: > strong for the diagnosis mechanism; moderate for the efficacy claim; weak for the specific 'doubles learning' wording. The mechanism claim is strong: it rests on Eedi's own help centre, Craig Barton's foundational writing, a decade of published misconception research, a publicly released large-scale response dataset and an independent peer-reviewed NeurIPS challenge built on it - and it is corroborated by the AI-tutor paper, which documents the pipeline feeding identified misconceptions into the model. The efficacy claim is moderate-to-strong by edtech standards specifically because of the active-control design (beating rival platforms, not beating nothing) and independent evaluation by WhatWorked Education plus an independent EduEvidence gold certification - but every one of these evaluates the WHOLE PLATFORM, and none isolates the causal contribution of the misconception diagnosis itself. The 'doubles the rate of learning in math' claim is weak as stated: it appears on marketing and third-party innovation-directory pages, is not traceable to the EEF trial (which produced no attainment outcome), and coexists awkwardly on the same page with a cited 0.17 SD effect and with the more recent and more modest 2-4 months figures. Treat it as marketing, cite the WhatWorked RCT and the EduEvidence certificate instead.
- **remediation_efficacy_evidence**: > Yes - the strongest in this research set, but with a precisely stateable limit. Positive: an independent randomised controlled trial (WhatWorked Education, 2023-24, 2,901 students, 20 UK schools) with an active control comparing Eedi against other leading digital maths platforms found 2 additional months of maths progress overall, 4 months for students using Eedi 10-15 minutes per week (113% of typical Year 7-8 annual progress), 3 months for below-median-attainment students and 3 months for Pupil Premium students with consistent use - i.e. the effect is largest for the students furthest behind, which is the population our product targets. An independent EduEvidence gold efficacy certificate rests on a two-year RCT with d=0.46 at 18 months and d=0.30 at 24 months. An earlier small pilot (4 schools, 12 KS3 classes) found 17% vs 6% post-test gains, d=0.50 for PP students. Separately, the Eedi Labs AI tutor RCT found students tutored by LearnLM were 5.5 percentage points more likely to solve novel problems on subsequent topics (66.2% vs 60.7%) than those receiving human tutoring alone, with equivalent immediate outcomes (93% vs 91.2%) - evidence that misconception-conditioned AI dialogue can produce transfer, not just local success. Negative/limits: the flagship EEF efficacy trial produced NO attainment estimate (Covid-19), so the largest study contributes nothing to the efficacy case; all studies evaluate the bundle rather than the diagnostic layer in isolation, so 'error-type diagnosis causes learning gains' remains formally unproven; the age bands studied are 11-15, not 9-11, so upper-primary transfer is an assumption; and none of it was conducted in a second-language or Indonesian context.

Nothing in the product distinguishes a misread instruction from a conceptual misunderstanding from an arithmetic slip - exactly the B/K/H distinction this market study is built on.
- **diagnosis_granularity**: > topic-mastery-map, backed by an unusually well-built prerequisite graph - but strictly one level above the granularity that matters. Concretely: (a) a skill network of 1000+ interconnected skills with explicit directed prerequisite edges, where 'An arrow from Skill A to Skill B indicates that Skill A is a prerequisite of Skill B'; (b) a 20-minute placement test on first entry to Adventure Island that sets a baseline; (c) a Student Mastery Report that classifies each topic/skill as 'Mastered' (4+ stars, high probability of success) or 'Not Mastered' (0-3 stars, indicating a potential knowledge gap), drillable from topic to sub-topic to underlying skill; (d) spiral learning / spaced revisiting of previously mastered skills. The output vocabulary is uniformly skills, topics, mastery and gaps. This is a better topic-mastery-map than most competitors - the prerequisite graph genuinely lets it walk BACKWARD to find a missing foundation, which is more than pure difficulty-throttling - but it still answers 'which skill is missing' rather than 'which kind of mistake is this child making'. On the field's own scale it sits at topic-mastery-map, not at mcq-distractor-mapped-misconception and nowhere near full-error-type-taxonomy.
- **diagnosis_input_modality**: > Interactive manipulative and game-action input, auto-scored - closest to a richer variant of mcq-distractor but WITHOUT distractor-to-misconception mapping. Children drag, sort, partition, place on number lines, and enter numeric answers inside purpose-built mini-games ('episodes'); Matific's pedagogy is explicitly 'learning by doing' with exploration in a playful setting rather than multiple-choice drill. Crucially, none of the following exist: free-response OCR of a photographed worksheet, handwriting-stroke capture, video-explained reasoning, or self-report of strategy. And although the game layer technically observes the child's interaction sequence, Matific's published engine description consumes only the OUTCOME of that sequence - activity completion and mastery demonstration - not its shape. That is the structural reason the engine cannot classify error type: the process signal is generated but discarded. Contrast with DreamBox, which claims to retain and act on the strategy trace.
- **ai_tutor_diagnosis_reliability**: > Not applicable in the failure-mode sense, because there is no error-type diagnosis to be unreliable - the honest finding is absence, not error. What Matific does have is an adaptive placement/routing engine whose reliability is validated only indirectly, through downstream achievement studies of the whole platform, never through any ground-truth comparison against expert-labelled misconceptions. Relevant structural weaknesses for this study: (1) the correct-answer trap is fully present - a lucky drag or a guessed numeric entry advances mastery identically to genuine understanding, since only outcomes are scored; (2) the 'Not Mastered' label is a probability-of-failure estimate, so it tells an adult that something is wrong but is silent on what, leaving the diagnostic inference entirely to a teacher or parent who may not have the mathematical-pedagogical training to make it; (3) the remediation move is mechanical - drop to prerequisite skills - which is the right response to a genuine foundational gap and the WRONG response to a reading error or a careless computation slip, yet the engine cannot tell these apart. There is no published validation of the placement test's accuracy, no reported reliability coefficients, and no independent audit of the skill network.
- **evidence_quote**: > The task's claim is CONFIRMED, verbatim, from an independent Australian tutoring-service review of primary maths apps: 'The adaptive algorithm adjusts difficulty, but it doesn't diagnose _why_ a child is struggling.' The same review continues: 'A student who can't add fractions might have a place value problem, a multiplication gap, or a conceptual misunderstanding about what fractions represent. Matific treats all errors the same - it just serves easier questions.' Its overall verdict: 'The best of the bunch for Australian curriculum alignment and conceptual visualisation. But it's a practice and reinforcement tool, not a teaching or diagnostic one.' This is corroborated - not contradicted - by Matific's own engine description, which never uses diagnostic vocabulary: 'The principal goal of the engine is to quickly identify a student's zone of proximal development (ZPD) and provide them with activities within that zone', and its remediation rule is purely structural - 'If a student is struggling with a particular skill, we can show students activities associated with its prerequisites.' A teacher review on Common Sense Education independently asked for the missing layer: more 'instructional feedback when kids get problems wrong'.

**Parent Role**

- **how_parent_involved**: > gatekeeper + dashboard-only, with a light assignment power - but never a coached participant. On the home track the parent is the account holder, payer and trial-starter (gatekeeper). The Parent Zone dashboard then gives: learning streak and weekly-goal progress, overall scores and count of completed activities, weekly summaries naming topics covered, and skill-specific performance reports framed as 'strengths and weaknesses'. Matific goes slightly further than most peers by letting the parent ASSIGN activities to target a weak area. But the entire exchange is numeric and topical - streak length, score, topics covered, weak skills - with no narrative of what the child actually got wrong, no explanation of why, and no script for what to say to the child. On the school track, which is Matific's dominant channel, the parent is closer to passive: the teacher assigns, and the parent watches a scoreboard for work they did not set. For the Indonesian parent persona in this study - who may not be able to interpret 'not mastered: partitioning fractions' unaided - a strengths-and-weaknesses skill list is not actionable guidance.
- **ai_tutor_directly_to_child**: > no. Matific has no conversational AI tutor and does not explain to the child in language. The child-facing feedback loop is the game itself: an episode reacts to actions, the manipulatives make the mathematical structure visible, and the design intent is that the child infers the concept from doing rather than from being told. Adventure Island adds narrative, character and reward framing around this, and the adaptive engine silently reroutes the child to easier prerequisite activities when they struggle - but nothing ever addresses the child about their specific mistake. This is a deliberate pedagogical stance (constructivist, manipulative-first, low-text, which is also what makes 50-language localization cheap), not an oversight - but it means the explanation layer that an Indonesian SD child on a borrowed phone would need is simply absent, from both the child side and the parent side.

**Evidence & Market Signal**

- **key_finding**: > The 'does not diagnose why' claim is VERIFIED and, more importantly, is structural rather than incidental - which makes Matific the cleanest benchmark for the gap this study is chasing. Matific has arguably the best-engineered prerequisite skill graph in primary edtech (1000+ skills with explicit directed prerequisite edges, a ZPD-targeting engine, a 20-minute placement test, spiral revisiting), it is genuinely multilingual including Bahasa Indonesia, it is priced within Indonesian reach, and it already has at least one real Indonesian school deployment. And it still cannot say whether a wrong answer was a reading error, a conceptual misconception or a calculation slip - because its engine consumes only outcomes, and its remediation vocabulary has exactly one verb: go back to the prerequisite. That single move is correct for a foundational gap and wrong for the other two error types, which is precisely the failure the B/K/H taxonomy is designed to prevent. Second key finding for the Indonesian question: Matific is NOT absent from Indonesia. Bahasa Indonesia is a natively supported language, Indonesia is one of the four target countries of the Matific Foundation's NIKI initiative (Nigeria, India, Kenya, Indonesia), and in 2025 Mentari Group / Mentari Assessment signed an MoU with Sekolah Yehonala in Batam to deploy Matific Math alongside Cambridge English Qualifications. - **evidence_strength**: > For the diagnosis-limitation claim: strong. It rests on an explicit independent verbatim statement, is corroborated by Matific's own engine documentation (which uses only skill/prerequisite/ZPD vocabulary and never misconception or error-type vocabulary), is corroborated again by the reporting layer's binary Mastered/Not-Mastered granularity, and is echoed by an independent teacher review asking for the missing instructional feedback on wrong answers. This matters symmetrically for the gap thesis: the absence of such evidence anywhere in the commercial market is simultaneously the opening (nobody has built it) and the risk (nobody has proven it works), and Matific - with the best prerequisite graph in the category and still no error-type layer - is the strongest single piece of evidence that the omission is a real product gap and not merely a small vendor's shortcoming.

### DreamBox / Discovery Education Math

<a id="dreambox-discovery-education-math"></a>
**Basic Info**

- **name**: DreamBox Math (DreamBox Learning, now DreamBox by Discovery Education)
- **category**: diagnostic_benchmark
- **target_age**: > K-8 for math (roughly ages 5-14), with the elementary K-5 band being the product's core and the source of essentially all its efficacy evidence. The SD-relevant band is K-6. DreamBox Reading is a separate K-5 product in the same suite.
- **delivery_mode**: > online-app. Browser plus iOS/Android apps. Two channels: school/district licensing (the dominant channel by far - DreamBox is fundamentally a district-sold supplemental intervention product) and a direct-to-family home subscription that unlocks the Family Dashboard. No tutoring, no human instructor, no offline component.
- **language_localization**: > No Bahasa Indonesia, and no path to it. DreamBox Math ships in English and Spanish only - Spanish exists specifically to serve US Spanish-speaking English Language Learners, with language-switching controls in both the educator dashboard and the Family Dashboard. For an Indonesian SD child this means every instruction, every prompt and every parent report is in English. Given that reading comprehension is one of the error types this study cares about, an English-only interface actively confounds the diagnosis it would otherwise be well positioned to make - the platform's strategy-level signal is real, but it cannot separate 'did not understand the mathematics' from 'did not understand the English'.
- **local_curriculum_alignment**: > None for Indonesia. DreamBox aligns to US Common Core State Standards and individual US state standards; its entire reporting layer (Standards Report, Standards Proficiency Report) is built on those standards, and its evidence packaging is built around ESSA tiers - a US federal funding framework. There is no Kurikulum Merdeka, Capaian Pembelajaran or K13 mapping, and no indication that one is planned. Sequencing, notation conventions and grade-band expectations follow US practice.

**Diagnosis Capability**

- **does_error_type_diagnosis**: > partial - and it is the strongest 'partial' in this competitor set, which makes it the most important benchmark in the study. DreamBox is genuinely different from Matific and IXL in ONE respect: it captures and acts on the child's problem-solving PROCESS, not only the final answer. Because activities are built on interactive virtual manipulatives, the engine observes the sequence of moves the child makes - how they decompose a number, which manipulative they reach for, how long they hesitate, whether they use an inefficient but valid strategy - and routes on that trace. Discovery Education's own curriculum-evaluation material states the engine responds to 'strategy, manipulative use, error patterns, decision sequences' and claims 'deeper misconception detection'. On the teacher side, the Lesson Highlights feature explicitly surfaces HOW a student solved a problem so teachers can 'target instruction to better address specific misconceptions'. That is materially beyond difficulty-and-topic adaptation. BUT it stops short of full error-type diagnosis in three decisive ways: (1) there is no published, named taxonomy of error types - no document lists the misconceptions the engine can identify, no category labels are exposed anywhere; (2) the engine's own OUTPUT is still a lesson recommendation and a standards-proficiency number, not a diagnostic label - the misconception inference stays internal and is never rendered as a statement about the child; (3) the parent-facing layer strips it out entirely, leaving progress, mastery and usage. So: adaptivity reaches strategy level, reporting does not.
- **diagnosis_granularity**: > Sits between topic-mastery-map and a genuine misconception map, and lands at different points depending on who is looking. Internally, the adaptive engine operates at strategy-trace granularity - it differentiates students who reached the same answer by different routes ('if three different students are working on a particular lesson, they will each be offered different follow-up lessons based on how they responded to questions throughout the lesson'), and reacts to 'repeated errors, hesitation, and inefficient strategies'. Placement is handled by a 'Launchpad placement engine' that starts each student at their just-right level. For TEACHERS, granularity is a standards-proficiency map (Standards Report and Standards Proficiency Report: percentage proficiency per standard, 100% only when every associated lesson is completed with demonstrated proficiency) PLUS Lesson Highlights, which is the one genuinely misconception-adjacent artifact in the product - but it works by showing the teacher the child's actual solution path and letting the TEACHER name the misconception, rather than naming it itself. For PARENTS, granularity collapses to topic-mastery-map at best: progress over week/month/year, lessons completed against a 5-lessons-per-week goal, and whether the child is mastering concepts. On the field's scale: strategy-informed topic-mastery-map for teachers, score-and-mastery for parents, and explicitly NOT a full-error-type-taxonomy for anyone.
- **diagnosis_input_modality**: > Interactive virtual-manipulative interaction sequences - a strategy trace - and this is DreamBox's real differentiator. The child works inside purpose-built manipulative environments (number racks, ten-frames, open number lines, area models) where the mathematics is performed rather than selected, so the engine records a sequence of actions and their timing, not a single submitted value. Vendor framing is 'insights from every interaction, not just right or wrong responses'. What is absent: no free-response OCR of a photographed worksheet, no handwriting-stroke capture, no video- or voice-explained reasoning, no self-report of strategy, and no MCQ-distractor-to-misconception mapping (DreamBox is largely not multiple choice, so distractor analysis does not apply). The practical limit: the trace is rich WITHIN the designed manipulative, and blind to anything the child does outside it - notably any working done on paper, which is where an Indonesian SD child's arithmetic slips actually live.
- **ai_tutor_diagnosis_reliability**: > The misconception-detection claim is vendor-asserted and has never been validated against ground truth in any source found here. The gap is specific and worth stating precisely: DreamBox has strong evidence that the PLATFORM raises test scores (multiple RCTs, an ESSA rating - see below) and NO evidence that its misconception inferences are CORRECT. No published study compares the engine's internal inferences against expert-labelled student misconceptions; no accuracy, precision or reliability figures are published; no taxonomy is published against which accuracy could even be defined. The phrases 'deeper misconception detection' and 'analyzes problem-solving approaches to understand conceptual gaps' appear in marketing and blog material, not in a technical or peer-reviewed description. Relevant failure modes: (1) the correct-answer trap is mitigated but not eliminated - DreamBox can distinguish an efficient strategy from a lucky one within a manipulative, which is a real advantage over answer-only platforms, but a child who guesses correctly inside a simple interaction still looks proficient; (2) hesitation and response-time signals, which the vendor says it uses, are notoriously confounded by distraction, device lag and shared-device conditions - directly relevant to a borrowed-Android use case; (3) the inference is never surfaced, so it is unfalsifiable in practice: no teacher or parent can see the engine's diagnosis and disagree with it, which removes the main real-world error-correction channel. Note also that several confident-sounding descriptions of DreamBox using Bayesian Knowledge Tracing and Item Response Theory come from low-quality AI-generated SEO blogs, not from DreamBox - those specific algorithmic claims should not be relied on.
- **evidence_quote**: > For the strategy-level adaptivity (the strongest pro-DreamBox evidence), from Discovery Education's own curriculum-evaluation Q&A: 'Its Intelligent Adaptive Engine responds in real time to every mathematical move a student makes (strategy, manipulative use, error patterns, decision sequences), adjusting instantly to their progress and performance' - claimed to yield 'deeper misconception detection', 'faster remediation' and 'transfer of learning'. From DreamBox's own support documentation: 'As students work through lessons using interactive virtual manipulatives, DreamBox Math captures, analyzes, and responds to each student's decisions, answers, and problem-solving strategies... if three different students are working on a particular lesson, they will each be offered different follow-up lessons based on how they responded to questions throughout the lesson.' For the one genuinely misconception-facing feature: 'Lesson Highlights provides a window into exactly how students are solving problems, allowing teachers to target instruction to better address specific misconceptions or misunderstandings' - note that the feature shows the teacher the solution path so the TEACHER can name the misconception; it does not label it. For the limit of the claim, the same evaluation document gives no examples of any specific misconception, error type or diagnostic protocol the system identifies, and no categorization scheme for student struggles beyond general references to 'thinking' and 'reasoning'. For the parent layer, the Family Dashboard is framed as: 'view changes in their understanding over the last week, month and year', 'how long your child has been using DreamBox - are they on track for their recommended goal of 5 lessons per week?', and 'Chat with your child about how they're using DreamBox and where they may need any help' - progress, usage and conversation prompts, with no error information at all.

**Parent Role**

- **how_parent_involved**: > gatekeeper + dashboard-only, with the diagnostic layer deliberately withheld. On the home subscription the parent is the payer and account holder (gatekeeper), and the Family Dashboard - which DreamBox states is 'available only with a Home Subscription' - provides real-time reports on activity, learning trends, gaps and growth, proficiency, and progress toward a recommended 5 lessons per week, plus goal-setting. DreamBox frames the parent explicitly as facilitator and motivator rather than instructor: 'Chat with your child about how they're using DreamBox and where they may need any help' and 'Make math a family affair'. The critical asymmetry for this study: DreamBox's most diagnostic artifact, Lesson Highlights - the one place where you can actually see HOW a child solved a problem - is a TEACHER-side feature and does not appear in the parent dashboard. The company evidently believes that interpreting a solution path requires professional training, and so gives parents a usage-and-mastery scoreboard instead. Furthermore, in the dominant district-sold channel the parent may have no dashboard at all, since Family Dashboard access is tied to the home subscription. Net: DreamBox's parent experience is progress-monitoring plus conversation-prompting, never explanation, and never any account of what specifically went wrong.
- **ai_tutor_directly_to_child**: > no. DreamBox has no conversational AI tutor and does not talk to the child in natural language about their mistakes. The child-facing experience is a gamified sequence of manipulative lessons in which the AI acts invisibly - it adjusts hints, scaffolding, difficulty, pacing and sequencing, and it selects the next lesson, but it never addresses the child about their reasoning. In-lesson support is pre-authored contextual hinting and adaptive scaffolding that is gradually withdrawn as understanding improves, tuned by the engine but not generated as an explanation of this child's specific error. Discovery Education's 2025 product announcements for DreamBox Math centred on accessibility improvements for K-5 and general personalization/engagement enhancements, not on a conversational tutor; no chatbot or generative explanation feature for DreamBox Math surfaced in this research. So despite having the best process-level signal in the category, DreamBox routes that signal into lesson selection and a teacher report - never into an explanation delivered to the learner.

**Evidence & Market Signal**

- **key_finding**: > DreamBox is the most important benchmark in this study because it is the closest anyone has come to error-type diagnosis at scale - and it still stops one step short, in a way that maps exactly onto the market gap. Answering the brief's core question directly: DreamBox's adaptivity goes BEYOND difficulty and topic. Because its activities are virtual manipulatives rather than answer boxes, the engine sees the child's solution PROCESS - strategy choice, manipulative use, hesitation, inefficient-but-valid routes - and two students who both answer correctly can be routed differently. That is genuinely deeper than Matific (outcome-only routing along a prerequisite graph) or IXL (answer-only, with a three-miss trouble-spot trigger). But three limits keep it out of the category this study is defining: (1) NO PUBLISHED TAXONOMY - 'misconception detection' is asserted repeatedly in marketing and never once instantiated with a named error type, a category list, or a worked example, and no validation against expert-labelled ground truth exists anywhere; (2) THE INFERENCE IS NEVER RENDERED - the engine's output is a lesson recommendation and a standards-proficiency percentage, so the diagnosis, if it exists, is consumed internally and never stated; (3) THE PARENT IS CUT OUT - the one feature that does expose the solution path, Lesson Highlights, is teacher-only, while the Family Dashboard is restricted to usage, mastery and 'chat with your child about where they may need help'. Layer on the Indonesian-market barriers - English and Spanish only, no Bahasa Indonesia, US Common Core alignment with nothing for Kurikulum Merdeka, roughly IDR 200,000/month, North-America-focused distribution with no visible Indonesian presence - and DreamBox is not a competitor in this market at all. Its value here is as proof: the best-funded, best-evidenced, most process-aware adaptive math engine in the world has the raw signal to diagnose error type, chooses not to expose it, and hands the parent a progress bar. The gap is not technical capability. It is product intent - nobody has built the layer that turns the process signal into a named error type and explains it to the child and the parent.
- **evidence_strength**: > Split, and the split is the point. For platform efficacy: strong by edtech standards but with small effects. Two randomized studies with significant positive results earn DreamBox a 'Strong' rating on Evidence for ESSA, and the 2022-23 LearnPlatform study satisfies ESSA Level III (Promising Evidence). Reported effects are modest: +0.16 effect size on the NWEA MAP Measurement and Geometry subtest and +0.11 overall in the Rocketship RCT; the Harvard CEPR study found the average student in a DreamBox classroom improved nearly 4 percentile points on NWEA MAP after only 14 hours of usage, and a related figure holds that 20 minutes/week yields about 2.5 NWEA MAP points. Note also the What Works Clearinghouse caveat attached to the ESSA listing that the review 'may not reflect the full body of research evidence for this intervention'. For the misconception-detection claim: weak/vendor-asserted. It appears only in Discovery Education marketing, blog and support material, is never instantiated with a taxonomy or example, and has zero independent validation. This asymmetry is the single most useful finding for the gap thesis: the efficacy evidence proves the bundle works, and proves nothing about the diagnostic mechanism the brief is asking about. A further caution: several of the most detailed-sounding public descriptions of DreamBox's algorithms (Bayesian Knowledge Tracing, IRT, 'targeted feedback tailored to the specific error') come from AI-generated SEO content farms and should be discounted entirely.
- **remediation_efficacy_evidence**: > Yes for the platform, no for the diagnosis - the same structural distinction as IXL, but with better platform evidence. DreamBox can point to two randomized controlled trials with significant positive results (Rocketship: 500+ K-1 students, +0.16 on the MAP Measurement and Geometry subtest, +0.11 overall; North Carolina: 24 schools, 10,000+ students grades K-2 and 4-5), a Harvard CEPR study of ~3,000 grade 3-5 students showing ~4 percentile points gain after 14 hours of use, a LearnPlatform 2022-23 evaluation meeting ESSA Level III, and a 'Strong' Evidence for ESSA rating. But every one of these tests the whole bundle - adaptive sequencing, manipulative-based instruction, contextual hinting, volume of practice, gamification, teacher assignment. None isolates the contribution of detecting WHAT KIND of mistake a student made, and none could, since DreamBox never emits an error-type label that could be experimentally manipulated or withheld. The practical implication for this study is important and cuts both ways: the claim 'diagnosing error type improves learning outcomes' has not been tested by the vendor best positioned to test it, and remains empirically open in the commercial market. What DreamBox's evidence DOES establish is a useful floor - a well-built adaptive math platform without error-type diagnosis produces real but small gains (effect sizes ~0.11-0.16), which is the bar any diagnosis-driven product would need to clear to justify its added complexity.

**Diagnosis Capability**

- **does_error_type_diagnosis**: > no - and this is now verified directly from ALEKS's own documentation and from the authoritative peer-reviewed account of its engine, not inferred. Knowledge Space Theory is a theory of WHICH items a student has mastered, not WHY a given response was wrong. The assessment output is a 'knowledge state': a subset of the domain's problem types (items) that the student most likely knows, plus the 'outer fringe' of items they are ready to learn next. Every item is resolved to one of three labels - in-state (likelihood above about 80 percent), out-of-state (below about 20 percent), or uncertain. There is no category for 'misread the question', 'holds misconception X', or 'arithmetic slip'. The check for a hidden error-type layer came back negative on all three probes: (a) ALEKS's own Knowledge Space Theory page and Research Behind ALEKS page never use the words misconception or error type; (b) the definitive technical paper (Cosyn, Uzun, Doble & Matayoshi, Journal of Mathematical Psychology, 2021) formalises response noise as exactly two scalar parameters per item - a careless error probability beta_q and a guessing probability eta_q - which are NOISE TERMS to be corrected for, never diagnostic outputs shown to anyone; (c) the wrong-answer path in Learning Mode serves a pre-authored 'Explanation' of how the problem should be solved for that problem type, i.e. a canonical worked solution, not a response to what this particular child did.
- **diagnosis_granularity**: > topic-mastery-map - but the finest-grained and most mathematically rigorous topic-mastery map in the entire competitor set, which is precisely why it is the sharpest available proof that a mastery map is not an error taxonomy. Granularity: a domain is decomposed into a few hundred problem types (about 400-500 core problem types for Algebra 1), and the combinatorial structure of feasible knowledge states is enormous (the College Placement knowledge structure contains roughly 10^23, about 2^77, states). ALEKS localises a student inside that structure in about 25-30 questions (initial assessment capped at 30, of which up to 29 are adaptively selected and one is a randomly chosen 'extra problem' used for validation). So the resolution is 'this child knows problem type #217 but not #218' - extremely fine on the WHICH axis, and completely silent on the WHY axis. Explicitly NOT mcq-distractor-mapped-misconception (ALEKS avoids MCQ by design) and NOT a full error-type taxonomy.
- **diagnosis_input_modality**: > free-response typed entry (open-ended answer input tools that mimic paper-and-pencil work: math palette, fraction/expression builders, graphing tools, calculator) plus one self-report channel - an 'I don't know' / 'I haven't learned it yet' button on every assessment question. Deliberately NOT mcq-distractor: the paper states plainly that 'the system avoids multiple choice formats'. There is no OCR of a photographed worksheet, no handwriting-stroke capture, no video or spoken explanation, and no capture of intermediate working - the student does the scratch work on paper and types only the final answer. This is the structural ceiling on ALEKS's diagnosis: the engine observes a final answer and a self-reported unfamiliarity flag, and nothing about the process that produced the answer, so it cannot classify error type even in principle. Notably, the free-response design does buy ALEKS one thing IXL and most MCQ platforms lack: because lucky guesses are near-zero, a correct answer is strong evidence of mastery - it defeats the guessing form of the correct-answer trap, but not the flawed-method-right-answer form.
- **ai_tutor_diagnosis_reliability**: > The state estimation is the best-validated adaptive assessment mechanism in this competitor set - and that only sharpens the point, because what is validated is mastery localisation, not error-type classification (of which there is none to validate). Validation design: every assessment includes a randomly selected 'extra problem' that is not used to update the state, so its observed correct rate can be compared against the model's prediction; across the dataset the extra-problem correct rate for an item tracks the lucky-guess rate eta_q on outer layers and 1 minus the careless-error rate beta_q on inner layers, confirming the Basic Local Independence Model's predictions - and also showing that beta and eta vary by knowledge-state layer rather than being constant, a known model limitation the authors flag. Documented failure modes relevant to us: (1) the 30-question cap means the assessment usually terminates before convergence, leaving 'uncertain' items excluded from the assigned state, so the initial state systematically UNDERESTIMATES the student's true state (ALEKS compensates by fast-tracking those items in Learning Mode); (2) a documented fatigue effect in long ALEKS assessments (Matayoshi et al. 2018); (3) careless errors are far more common than lucky guesses in ALEKS because items are open-ended, so a mastered item can still be answered wrong and be silently absorbed as noise - the child's slip is modelled away rather than reported to anyone; (4) the update after an incorrect response must be deliberately non-aggressive because of that careless-error rate, which is exactly why the 'I don't know' button had to be added as a workaround. In short: ALEKS knows careless errors exist, measures their rate precisely, and treats them as statistical nuisance rather than as diagnostic information for the teacher or parent.
- **evidence_quote**: > From Cosyn, Uzun, Doble & Matayoshi, 'A practical perspective on knowledge space theory: ALEKS and its data' (Journal of Mathematical Psychology, 2021): 'Note that, because of the probabilistic nature of the assessment procedure, this final state may contain an item to which the student gave an incorrect response. Such a response is regarded as due to a careless error. ALEKS items typically have open-ended answers, in the sense that the system avoids multiple choice formats and instead uses answer input tools that mimic what would be done with paper and pencil. As such, the lucky guess probability may be assumed to be very small.' And on the formal model: 'For each q in Q, there are two constants beta_q, eta_q in [0,1[, respectively called (careless) error probability and guessing probability at q' - the entire response-error model is two scalars per item, 'with no' further structure. On the output: assessment yields 'the precise and comprehensive description of an individual student's competence' plus 'the problem types that the individual is ready to learn next'. Note the noun throughout: problem types and states, never kinds of mistakes. From ALEKS's own Master Account page, the complete parent feature list: 'Change your name, address, or password'; 'Check your automatic payments'; 'Change your billing information'; 'Change the grade, topic, and password on your student account(s)'; 'View the progress and latest assessment report for each student account'.

### Newman's Error Analysis (1977) & VanLehn slip/bug/misconception taxonomy (1983)

<a id="newmans-error-analysis-1977-vanlehn-slipbugmisconception-taxonomy-1983"></a>
**Basic Info**

- **name**: > Newman's Error Analysis (Newman 1977, 1983) and the VanLehn slip / bug / misconception taxonomy (Brown & VanLehn 1980; VanLehn 1983, 1990)
- **category**: academic_framework
- **origin_region**: > Two independent lineages. (1) Newman's Error Analysis (NEA): Australia - Anne Newman, Victoria, published in the Victorian Institute for Educational Research Bulletin (1977) and as 'Strategies for Diagnosis and Remediation' (Harcourt Brace Jovanovich, Sydney, 1983); propagated by M. A. (Ken) Clements and Nerida Ellerton through the 1980s-90s across Australia, Malaysia and Brunei Darussalam, revived in New South Wales in 2007 inside the NSW Department of Education 'Counting On' program. (2) VanLehn: United States - Repair Theory developed by John Seely Brown and Kurt VanLehn at Xerox PARC (Cognitive Science, 1980), formalised in VanLehn (1983) and consolidated in 'Mind Bugs: The Origins of Procedural Misconceptions' (MIT Press, 1990), with the BUGGY/DEBUGGY diagnostic systems and the Sierra learning model. Highly relevant to us: NEA has become the single most-used error-analysis framework in Indonesian mathematics education research, where it appears in journal after journal as 'prosedur Newman' / 'Newman's Error Analysis' applied to soal cerita and AKM items at SD and SMP level.
- **target_age**: > NEA: originally sixth-grade pupils (Newman 1977 analysed sixth-graders' errors on written mathematical tasks); Clements' replications covered Grades 5-8; NSW Counting On applied it to primary Years 5-6 and secondary Years 7-9. It is fundamentally a word-problem framework, so it applies from the moment a child reads mathematics rather than just computes it - roughly SD kelas 2-3 upward, exactly our band. VanLehn: multi-column subtraction in Grades 2-5 (the empirical core), extended to algebra mal-rules for older students.
- **delivery_mode**: > offline-tutoring for NEA, computational for VanLehn - and this split is the central strategic fact. NEA is executed as a one-to-one structured diagnostic INTERVIEW: an adult sits with the child and issues five spoken prompts while the child works. It has never been packaged as software. VanLehn's lineage is the opposite: it was born computational (BUGGY, DEBUGGY, Sierra) and diagnoses from written answer patterns without an interview, which is why it, and not NEA, is the branch that made it into shipping intelligent tutoring systems.
- **pricing_model**: > free - both are open academic frameworks in the public literature with no licence, no vendor and no per-seat cost. Newman's five prompts can be printed on a poster (NSW teachers literally did this). The economic implication is that neither framework is defensible as intellectual property; the defensibility of any product built on them lies entirely in the operationalisation - capturing enough of the child's process to classify reliably, and turning the classification into something a parent can act on.
- **local_curriculum_alignment**: > Not curriculum-bound, and that is an advantage: NEA classifies the PROCESS of solving a word problem, so it overlays any curriculum, including Kurikulum Merdeka and AKM-style numeracy items. Indonesian studies already apply Newman's procedure specifically to AKM content (e.g. analysis of SD students' errors on AKM data-and-probability items using prosedur Newman), which is direct evidence that the framework maps cleanly onto current Indonesian assessment framing without adaptation.

**Diagnosis Capability**

- **does_error_type_diagnosis**: > yes - unambiguously, for both. These are not systems that happen to reveal error type; classifying error type IS what they are. NEA holds that answering a written word problem requires clearing five successive hurdles, and that a wrong answer must be attributed to the FIRST hurdle the child failed: (1) Reading/Decoding, (2) Comprehension, (3) Transformation, (4) Process Skills, (5) Encoding - plus two orthogonal categories Newman added because they cut across the hierarchy: Careless errors and errors caused by lack of Motivation. The mapping onto B/K/H is close to one-to-one: B (salah Baca) = Reading + Comprehension; K (salah Konsep) = Transformation; H (salah Hitung) = Process Skills + Encoding + Careless. VanLehn's taxonomy is the complementary axis, distinguishing three fundamentally different things a wrong answer can mean: a SLIP (a non-systematic, unintended performance error - our H), a BUG (a systematic, reproducible but incorrect procedure that the child executes faithfully, e.g. always subtracting the smaller digit from the larger regardless of position - our K, at procedural level), and a MISCONCEPTION (the underlying flawed knowledge that generates the bug). The generative mechanism is impasse-and-repair: a child's procedure is incomplete, hits an impasse on a problem it cannot handle, and applies a 'repair' - a local patch - which, when it produces wrong output, manifests as a bug. Together the two frameworks give exactly what a diagnosis engine needs: NEA says WHERE in the solving pipeline the failure occurred, VanLehn says WHAT KIND of cognitive object caused it and whether it will recur.
- **diagnosis_granularity**: > full-error-type-taxonomy - the reference standard against which every commercial product in this study is being measured. NEA yields a 5-level ordinal classification plus careless/motivation flags (in the NSW Counting On evaluation a sixth category was appended for students who solved the problem successfully, giving a usable 1-6 scale). VanLehn yields a discrete bug library: more than 200 distinct bugs were catalogued for multi-column subtraction alone, with bugs frequently co-occurring in combinations, and each bug is a fully specified procedure so it predicts exactly which future problems the child will get wrong. Critically, Ellerton and Clements (1997) extended NEA beyond wrong answers: analysing responses to 46 questions from Grades 5-8, they 'challenged the view that correct answers equated to understanding', discussed correct and incorrect responses alike, and assigned a Newman error category to a CORRECT answer when the analysis showed it was not accompanied by adequate understanding. That is a 1997 statement of what the 2026 AI-tutor literature now calls the 'correct answer trap', and it is a direct mandate for diagnosing process rather than outcome.
- **diagnosis_input_modality**: > self-report / think-aloud interview for NEA; written-work step analysis for VanLehn. NEA's instrument is five scripted prompts delivered orally while the child works: '1. Please read the question to me. If you don't know a word, leave it out. 2. Tell me what the question is asking you to do. 3. Tell me how you are going to find the answer. 4. Show me what to do to get the answer. Talk aloud as you do it, so that I can understand how you are thinking. 5. Now, write down your answer to the question.' Prompt 1 isolates decoding, prompt 2 comprehension, prompt 3 transformation, prompt 4 process skills, prompt 5 encoding. Note the modality implication for a product: prompts 1 and 4 require CAPTURING THE CHILD'S VOICE, and prompt 4 requires capturing intermediate working - neither of which any mastery-map platform (ALEKS, IXL) collects. VanLehn's DEBUGGY works from a different channel: it fits a bug library to the pattern of answers across a carefully designed problem set, requiring no interview at all, which makes it automatable but blind to reading and comprehension failures. A modern implementation would combine them: OCR or handwriting capture of working for the VanLehn axis, plus a spoken or tapped re-tell for the Newman reading/comprehension axis.
- **ai_tutor_diagnosis_reliability**: > No AI is involved in either original framework - reliability concerns are of two kinds. (A) Intrinsic to the frameworks. VanLehn's own most important negative finding is BUG INSTABILITY and BUG MIGRATION: a bug observed in a child on one occasion frequently fails to reappear on later items or in a later session. VanLehn's explanation is that the underlying incomplete procedure is stable, but the child selects a different REPAIR at the impasse each time, so the surface bug migrates. The practical consequence is severe and directly relevant to us: a single-shot diagnosis of 'this child has bug X' is unreliable, and later work had to introduce probabilistic methods (e.g. Bayesian-network diagnosis of subtraction bugs) precisely to handle instability. Any product claiming an error-type label from one observation is overclaiming; the label needs repeated evidence and an explicit confidence. NEA has its own reliability caveat: it depends on an adult conducting the interview faithfully, and Casey's (1978) modification - where interviewers helped students past each error so a single question could yield multiple error codes - produces classifications that are not comparable with Newman's original single-first-hurdle coding. So even the human gold standard has coding-protocol variance. (B) Extrinsic, for anyone automating this today. Recent work is directly on point: a 2026 study using 20,964 real student responses from the Eedi mathematics platform found that fine-tuned classifiers detect only 57 percent of hidden misconceptions where flawed reasoning produced a CORRECT answer, that standard ML interventions do not improve on this, and that an open-weight reasoning model reaches 84 percent detection but at realistic prevalence produces false alarms outnumbering genuine detections roughly 8 to 1. Separately, LAK 2026 work on automated assessment of handwritten mathematics found LLMs (Gemini strongest) achieve strong-to-moderate performance at diagnosing and classifying student errors against a math-error taxonomy, but that in the best model 87 percent of grading errors were TRANSCRIPTION failures rather than misapplied rubric - i.e. the bottleneck for an OCR-based error-type product is reading the child's handwriting correctly, not reasoning about the error once read.
- **evidence_quote**: > Newman's five prompts, verbatim from White (2009): '1. Please read the question to me. If you don't know a word, leave it out. 2. Tell me what the question is asking you to do. 3. Tell me how you are going to find the answer. 4. Show me what to do to get the answer. "Talk aloud" as you do it, so that I can understand how you are thinking. 5. Now, write down your answer to the question.' On the framework's purpose: 'Rather than give students "more of the same" involving drill and practice, NEA provided a framework for considering the reasons that underlay the difficulties and a process that assisted teachers to determine where misunderstandings occurred and where to target effective teaching strategies to overcome them.' On the correct-answer trap, 1997: Ellerton and Clements 'challenged the view that correct answers equated to understanding, thus all responses, both correct and incorrect, were discussed. A correct answer which, after analysis, was not deemed to be associated with an adequate understanding of the main concepts, and/or skills and/or relationships tested by a question, would be associated with a Newman error category, even though the answer was correct.' On the empirical distribution of error types, Clements (1980), 6595 errors from 634 children: '70% of errors belonged to reading, comprehension, transformation or carelessness categories. The first three accounted for 40% and there were approximately 30% careless errors. The mathematics procedure level of process skills registered 25% and encoding only accounted for about 5%.' On VanLehn's mechanism: bugs arise when 'a procedure cannot be performed, an impasse occurs and the individual applies various strategies to overcome the impasse; these strategies (meta-actions) are called repairs' - and systematic bugs 'are not to be confused with slips'. On instability: 'bugs are unstable, meaning that a bug symptom observed in an individual might not show up in subsequent items or in a subsequent testing session.'

**Parent Role**

- **how_parent_involved**: > none - and this is the single largest unfilled space the two frameworks leave open. Both were built for and by professionals. NEA's interviewer is explicitly a teacher or researcher; the NSW Counting On deployment routed everything through teacher professional learning, classroom posters of the five prompts, and teacher-led remediation, with parents nowhere in the design. VanLehn's audience was cognitive scientists and ITS builders; a bug library is unreadable to a layperson. Neither framework produces any parent-facing artefact, any script for what a parent should say to a child, or any account of how a non-expert adult could run the diagnosis. Yet the five prompts are strikingly parent-executable - they are five plain-language questions requiring no mathematical expertise from the asker, only the willingness to listen. Forty-nine years after Newman, nobody appears to have packaged them for parents. That is a designed-in gap, not a technical barrier.
- **ai_tutor_directly_to_child**: > no. In NEA the entity addressing the child is a human interviewer following a script; the framework's entire diagnostic power comes from a person listening to a child think aloud. VanLehn's DEBUGGY spoke to no one at all - it produced a bug diagnosis for a researcher or a tutoring system, not an explanation for the learner. The child-facing implementations came later and derivatively: Cognitive Tutor / MATHia surfaces buggy-production feedback messages to the student, but as templated text attached to a recognised buggy rule, not as dialogue. Neither framework specifies, or has ever required, an AI talking to a child - which means an implementation is free to route the diagnosis either to the child or to the parent.

**Evidence & Market Signal**

- **key_finding**: > These two frameworks are the strongest possible theoretical footing for a B/K/H taxonomy, and the state of their operationalisation is the market gap itself. Three findings matter. FIRST, the theory is settled and the empirical distribution of error types actively vindicates a B/K/H design. Clements (1980), analysing 6,595 errors from 634 children, found that only about 25 percent of errors were process-skill (computational) failures and about 5 percent encoding, while 40 percent were reading, comprehension or transformation failures and 30 percent were careless. In other words, roughly seven out of ten mathematics errors are NOT arithmetic errors - they happen before the child ever computes anything. Clements and Ellerton went on to publish this across five countries under the title 'Overemphasising process skills in school mathematics', which is precisely the critique that applies to every drill-and-practice app in this competitive set: they remediate the 25 percent and are structurally blind to the 70 percent. SECOND, NEA has never been operationalised as software. Repeated targeted searching found NEA embedded in teacher professional learning (NSW Counting On, the AMIC program in Brunei), in classroom posters, and in a very large Indonesian academic literature - but in no shipping product, no app, and no automated classifier. It remains a manual, interview-based, one-child-at-a-time procedure that does not scale, which is exactly why it has not displaced score-based edtech despite being demonstrably better diagnosis. THIRD, the branch that DID ship is VanLehn's, and only in its narrowest form: Carnegie Learning's Cognitive Tutor and MATHia encode common misconceptions as 'buggy' production rules within a model-tracing engine, so that a recognised buggy step triggers a specific message explaining what is wrong, and Eedi (UK) ships the cheap approximation - every question has three distractors, each pre-mapped to a named misconception, so a single click identifies the error. Both operationalisations are severely constrained: buggy production rules only fire on procedural steps the author anticipated inside a structured interface, and distractor mapping only works for multiple choice and cannot see reading errors at all. Neither captures the Newman axis. The synthesis for our thesis: the theory says most primary-mathematics errors are reading and comprehension failures; the only commercially operationalised error-type diagnosis covers procedural and conceptual bugs via MCQ distractors or step tracing; and nobody has built the reading/comprehension half, nobody has automated Newman's five prompts, and nobody has ever pointed any of it at a parent.
- **study_scale_or_sample**: > NEA: Newman (1977) analysed sixth-grade pupils' errors on written mathematical tasks in Victoria, Australia. Clements (1980) reported two large studies in which 6,595 errors made by 634 children were analysed using NEA, replicating Newman's distribution. Casey (1978) studied 120 Grade 7 students in a single high school. Ellerton and Clements (1997) analysed Grades 5-8 responses to a set of 46 questions. Clements and Ellerton (1992) reported Newman analysis data from five countries. NSW Counting On 2008: implemented in 99 schools, data collected from 74 schools (55 primary, 16 secondary, 3 central) covering 1,213 students (954 primary, 78.6 percent; 259 secondary, 21.4 percent), each assessed pre and post on the NEA 1-6 scale around 10 weeks of targeted activity; the 2007 revision had run across 122 schools in 30 clusters. Indonesian replication literature is extensive but consists of many small qualitative studies (typically one class, descriptive-qualitative design) rather than large samples. VanLehn: more than 200 distinct subtraction bugs described in VanLehn (1990); predictions tested against error data from several thousand subjects in schools worldwide; one frequently reanalysed subtraction dataset covers 520 examinees on a single test form, drawn from Grades 3-5 in two southern San Francisco Bay Area school districts. Modern automation evidence: 20,964 real student responses from Eedi in the 2026 correct-answer-trap study.
- **evidence_strength**: > strong for the descriptive claim, moderate for the causal claim - and the two must not be conflated. Strong: that mathematics errors decompose into reliably distinguishable types, and that reading/comprehension/transformation failures dominate over computational ones, is supported by thousands of coded errors, cross-national replication across five countries, half a century of use, and an enormous independent Indonesian replication literature. VanLehn's bug catalogue is likewise supported by data from several thousand subjects and was rigorously formalised. Moderate to weak: that ACTING on the diagnosis improves outcomes. The best evidence for NEA remediation is a single-group pre-post evaluation with no control condition (below), and VanLehn's own finding of bug instability undermines confident single-shot diagnosis. The framework's descriptive validity is not in question; its causal payoff has never been tested to modern standards.

### Cognitive Tutor / ASSISTments (buggy-rules ITS research)

<a id="cognitive-tutor-assistments-buggy-rules-its-research"></a>
**Basic Info**

- **name**: > Cognitive Tutor / MATHia (ACT-R buggy-rule model tracing) and ASSISTments - the buggy-rules ITS research tradition
- **category**: academic_framework
- **origin_region**: > Global, USA-originated. Two linked but institutionally separate lineages. (1) The ACT-R / Cognitive Tutor lineage: John R. Anderson's ACT-R lab at Carnegie Mellon University (Pittsburgh), running from the LISP Tutor and Geometry Tutor of the mid-1980s through the Algebra Cognitive Tutor, commercialised via the CMU spin-out Carnegie Learning (founded 1998) and sold today as MATHia. Its intellectual ancestors are Brown & Burton's BUGGY/DEBUGGY (1978, elementary multi-column subtraction) and Sleeman's PIXIE/LMS mal-rule work (UK/Stanford/Aberdeen). (2) The ASSISTments lineage: Neil Heffernan and Cristina Heffernan at Worcester Polytechnic Institute (Massachusetts), launched around 2003 as a free non-profit platform, with efficacy trials run by SRI International and WestEd and funded by NSF, IES and Arnold Ventures. Both are US school-system products; neither has any Indonesian presence, distributor or localisation programme.
- **delivery_mode**: > online-app, school-mediated and classroom-blended - not a direct-to-consumer parent purchase. Cognitive Tutor Algebra I / MATHia is designed as a blended curriculum: roughly two days a week of individual software work inside a textbook-plus-classroom sequence, delivered on school desktops or Chromebooks, adopted at district level. ASSISTments is a free web platform that a teacher assigns as homework or classwork; the Maine trial worked because the state issued a laptop to every 7th grader. Neither is a mobile-first app, neither is designed for a shared or borrowed Android phone, and neither functions without a teacher in the loop to assign work and read the reports. This is the single biggest deployment-model gap versus an Indonesian parent-purchased home product.
- **local_curriculum_alignment**: > None for Indonesia. Both are aligned to US Common Core State Standards and to state frameworks (Carnegie Learning maintains California, Texas and Florida editions and submits to state adoption reviews). There is no Kurikulum Merdeka or K13 mapping, no CP/ATP alignment, no Indonesian textbook coverage and no fase-based sequencing. Their transferable asset for an Indonesian thesis is the METHOD (buggy production rules, step-level model tracing, mastery learning, common-wrong-answer surfacing), not the content.

**Diagnosis Capability**

- **does_error_type_diagnosis**: > yes for Cognitive Tutor / MATHia at the strongest level available in the field; partial for ASSISTments. Cognitive Tutor is the canonical positive case: its cognitive model is an ACT-R production system containing both CORRECT production rules (often several, one per valid strategy) and BUGGY productions that encode specific known misconceptions. At every step the tutor runs model tracing - it attempts to explain the student's observed action as a firing of some production in the model. If the action matches a correct production, the step is accepted and the corresponding knowledge component's mastery estimate is updated via knowledge tracing. If it matches a BUGGY production, the tutor knows not merely THAT the step is wrong but WHICH misconception produced it, and fires a pre-authored 'buggy message' explaining the specific error. This is genuine error-TYPE classification, performed on the student's own intermediate work, at step granularity - architecturally the closest published analogue to a B/K/H engine. ASSISTments is weaker and more pragmatic: its per-step judgement is correctness-based (right/wrong) with hints and scaffolding questions triggered on error, and its error-TYPE layer is the 'Common Wrong Answers' (CWA) report, which surfaces to the TEACHER any incorrect answer given by three or more students, plus QUICK-Comments, a repository of pre-authored comments addressing common errors. So ASSISTments detects error PATTERNS statistically and hands them to a human, rather than classifying an individual child's error causally.
- **diagnosis_granularity**: > Cognitive Tutor / MATHia: full-error-type-taxonomy, and additionally a topic-mastery-map. The buggy production library is a hand-authored taxonomy of named misconceptions (for example a buggy production that fails to distribute an operation fully across an expression), and it operates at the level of individual solution STEPS, not whole answers. Layered on top is knowledge tracing over knowledge components, producing the per-skill mastery bars that drive mastery-learning progression. ASSISTments: trouble-spot-quantity plus topic-mastery-map, with a distractor/common-wrong-answer overlay - the teacher report shows which items and which skills the class struggled with, which specific wrong answers clustered, and Skill Builders enforce mastery thresholds. Critically, neither system reaches the granularity a B/K/H taxonomy needs on ONE dimension: neither separates a READING/comprehension failure from a CONCEPT failure from a CALCULATION slip as an explicit orthogonal taxonomy. Cognitive Tutor's buggy rules are procedural-misconception labels tied to specific problem types; they are numerous and specific but not organised into a small, parent-legible causal taxonomy. That organisational move - collapsing hundreds of item-specific bugs into three actionable causes a parent can act on - is precisely the unoccupied design space.
- **diagnosis_input_modality**: > Structured step-entry in a constrained tutor interface - NOT handwriting, and this is the load-bearing limitation. Cognitive Tutor requires the student to type intermediate steps into a worksheet-like grid, algebra tool or equation solver whose input format the model-tracer can parse deterministically. Model tracing only works because the interface forces the student's reasoning into machine-readable slots; the price is that the child must translate their thinking into the tutor's representation, and the tutor sees nothing the interface did not capture. ASSISTments uses typed short answers and multiple choice, with the CWA layer effectively an mcq-distractor / free-response-string clustering. Neither ingests handwriting strokes, neither photographs a child's exercise book, and neither observes the working a child actually produces with pencil on paper. For an Indonesian SD product this matters twice over: kelas 4 children do their real mathematical thinking on paper, and typing structured algebraic steps on a borrowed phone is not a viable input channel. Handwriting-stroke capture of naturally produced step-by-step working is therefore a modality this entire tradition has never used - it gets step-level evidence, which VanLehn's meta-review shows is what actually matters, but it pays for that evidence with an artificial input interface that an elementary home product cannot replicate.
- **ai_tutor_diagnosis_reliability**: > Well validated as a MECHANISM, but with three documented and severe limitations that a B/K/H product must design around. (1) COVERAGE. Buggy rule libraries are hand-authored by cognitive scientists, one problem type at a time; they are expensive, and they only recognise errors someone anticipated. Any error not in the library is simply 'wrong' with no type attached, which means the diagnostic taxonomy silently degrades to score-only on the long tail. (2) TRANSFER. This is the most damaging finding. Payne & Squibb (1990, Cognitive Science 14:445-481, 'Algebra Mal-Rules and Cognitive Accounts of Error') established that mal-rule / bug libraries DO NOT TRANSFER WELL BETWEEN DIFFERENT POPULATIONS OF STUDENTS - a library calibrated on one group of learners loses much of its explanatory coverage on another. The direct implication for Indonesia is that a bug taxonomy imported from US algebra research cannot be assumed valid for Indonesian kelas 4 arithmetic; it must be re-derived from Indonesian children's actual work. (3) INSTABILITY. The broader literature on diagnostic representation of algebra errors repeatedly finds student errors are less stable across occasions than the bug-library model assumes - the same child may not apply the same mal-rule consistently, which caps achievable diagnostic reliability regardless of engineering effort. This is why the field partly moved to constraint-based modelling (Ohlsson, Mitrovic), which checks violated constraints rather than trying to name the buggy procedure, precisely because enumerating bugs proved brittle. On the positive side, model tracing itself is validated: it is deterministic given the model, and the knowledge-tracing mastery estimates it feeds have been repeatedly shown to predict performance, so the system's failure mode is under-coverage and mislabelling, not fabrication - a meaningfully safer profile than an LLM that will confidently invent a misconception.
- **evidence_quote**: > From Anderson, Corbett, Koedinger & Pelletier, 'Cognitive Tutors: Lessons Learned' (Journal of the Learning Sciences, 1995), the mechanism is stated directly: 'If the student makes a recognizable error (a bug) a message can be given explaining why it is an error. This is generated from a buggy production that embodies the error.' And on the authoring cost: 'Error Messages. When the student makes an error one can present a message that attempts to tell the student something useful about that error. This requires writing buggy productions and attaching instruction from these productions.' On the cautionary side, the field's own summary of Sleeman, Kelly, Martinak, Ward & Moore (1989) is that 'providing remedial feedback tuned to bugs is no more effective than instruction that simply reteaches the correct approach.' And on the ASSISTments side, the platform describes Common Wrong Answers as triggering when 'three or more students provided the same incorrect answer to a problem, highlighting key areas of student misunderstanding' - i.e. a teacher-facing statistical signal, not an individual causal diagnosis.

**Parent Role**

- **how_parent_involved**: > none to passive - the parent is essentially unmodelled in both systems, and this is a structural gap, not an oversight. Both are institution-facing: the data consumer is the TEACHER, not the parent. Cognitive Tutor / MATHia reports go to teacher dashboards and district administrators; Carnegie Learning's implementation model is district professional development. ASSISTments' entire causal story runs through the teacher - the Roschelle et al. (2016) trial explicitly concluded that 'teacher access to student data was an important mechanism' and that teachers 'targeted their homework reviews to focus on student difficulties and errors'. A parent may see a grade; they do not receive a diagnostic report, they are not given remediation scripts, and there is no parent account with its own view. For an Indonesian product where there IS no teacher in the loop and the parent is the only available adult, this tradition supplies the diagnostic engine but supplies nothing at all for the delivery layer - the entire 'who reads the diagnosis and acts on it' problem has to be solved from scratch.
- **ai_tutor_directly_to_child**: > yes, but non-conversationally, and with an important design lesson. Cognitive Tutor talks directly to the child at every step: it flags the erroneous step immediately, fires the specific buggy message for recognised misconceptions, offers a multi-level on-demand hint sequence escalating from a conceptual nudge to a bottom-out hint that states the answer, and gates progression on mastery. ASSISTments likewise gives the child immediate correctness feedback plus on-request hints and scaffolding sub-questions that decompose the problem. But neither is an LLM chat - all messages are pre-authored and template-selected, so the child cannot ask a follow-up, cannot say 'I still don't understand', and receives nothing outside what an author anticipated. The design lesson from the associated help-seeking literature (Aleven, Roll, McLaren & Koedinger, 'Help Helps, But Only So Much') is that direct-to-child help is not automatically beneficial: students game the system by drilling to bottom-out hints, and help-avoidance and help-abuse are both common failure modes. Putting a tutor voice in front of a child is necessary but not sufficient; what the child DOES with it determines whether it helps.

**Evidence & Market Signal**

- **key_finding**: > This tradition is simultaneously the strongest existence proof that step-level error-type diagnosis is buildable and works, AND the strongest published warning that DIAGNOSIS ALONE BUYS NOTHING. Four findings should anchor the thesis. FIRST, and most important as a caution: Sleeman, Kelly, Martinak, Ward & Moore (1989, Cognitive Science 13(4):551-568, 124 citations) ran three controlled studies comparing model-based remediation (MBR) - where the system infers the individual student's specific mal-rules and remediates those bugs - against plain RETEACHING of the correct procedure. MBR was NOT reliably better than simply reteaching. Bunderson & Olsen (1983) had already found no difference between error-specific remediation and reteaching; only Swan (1983), using a CONFLICT approach that actively confronted the student with the consequences of their error rather than merely naming it, beat reteaching. The lesson is precise and directly usable: naming the bug to the learner is not the active ingredient. What differentiates is the remediation ACTIVITY - specifically cognitive-conflict activities that make the error's wrongness visible to the child - not the diagnostic label. A B/K/H product that ships a beautiful taxonomy and a parent report saying 'your child's error was Conceptual' has, on this evidence, shipped the inert half. SECOND, and this is the correction to the framing of this research request: there is NO large-scale ASSISTments RCT showing that error-diagnosis messages fail. The large ASSISTments RCTs are POSITIVE - Roschelle, Feng, Murphy & Mason (2016, AERA Open) found g = 0.18 with 2,850 seventh graders across 43 Maine schools, and the North Carolina replication (63 schools) found sustained effects a year later at the end of 8th grade (effect size around 0.10). What those trials actually show is subtler and more useful: the benefit was attributed to the FULL package - immediate feedback plus hints plus scaffolding plus, crucially, teacher use of the error data to retarget instruction - not to error messages in isolation. THIRD, the ASSISTments team's own meta-analysis of its internal experiment platform makes the diagnosis-versus-remediation contrast quantitatively: Prihar, Syed, Ostrow, Shaw, Sales & Heffernan (EDM 2022, 'Exploring Common Trends in Online Educational Experiments') aggregated 50 randomised experiments covering 30,408 unique students and found that five of six TUTORING strategies significantly outperformed the 'answer only' condition. In other words, telling a student what was wrong is the weak baseline; supplying actual instructional content on top of it is what produces measurable gains. FOURTH, the modality argument in the thesis's favour: VanLehn's (2011, Educational Psychologist 46(4):197-221) meta-review found human tutoring at d = 0.79, STEP-BASED intelligent tutoring at d = 0.76 - statistically indistinguishable from a human tutor - but ANSWER-BASED tutoring at only d = 0.31. Seeing the student's intermediate steps is worth roughly 2.5x an answer-only system. That is the single strongest empirical justification available for capturing handwritten step-by-step working rather than grading final answers, and it comes from outside the AI hype cycle. Synthesised design implication for a B/K/H product: (a) build the step-level capture, because VanLehn shows that is where the effect size lives; (b) do NOT expect the diagnosis label itself to move outcomes, because Sleeman shows it does not; (c) pair every diagnosis with a specific remediation ACTIVITY, preferring cognitive-conflict tasks and contrasting cases that let the child see their rule break, over explanatory messages; (d) re-derive the error taxonomy on Indonesian children's actual work rather than importing one, because Payne & Squibb showed bug libraries do not transfer across populations; (e) keep the taxonomy small and causally organised (B/K/H) rather than a sprawling item-specific bug library, because the sprawling version is exactly what proved unmaintainable and non-transferable; and (f) accept that the entire tradition solved the diagnosis problem for a TEACHER audience and left the parent-delivery problem completely untouched.
- **evidence_strength**: > strong. This is the best-evidenced body of work in the entire competitor and framework set. It rests on multi-site cluster-randomised trials with thousands of students (RAND/Pane; SRI/Roschelle), an independent replication with long-term follow-up (WestEd/Feng), What Works Clearinghouse review of Cognitive Tutor, a 50-experiment internal meta-analysis, and a widely cited meta-review of tutoring effect sizes (VanLehn). The cautionary findings are somewhat older and smaller-n (Sleeman 1989 is three studies, not a mega-trial; Payne & Squibb 1990 is a corpus study) and so carry moderate rather than strong individual weight - but they have survived thirty-five years of citation without being overturned, and the EDM 2022 meta-analysis independently reproduces the same directional conclusion with modern data. The one framing that must NOT be asserted is that a large RCT proved error-diagnosis messages fail; the honest and still-powerful claim is that error-specific remediation has repeatedly failed to beat ordinary reteaching, while richer tutoring content reliably beats correctness-only feedback.
- **remediation_efficacy_evidence**: > Yes, and it is the most decision-relevant evidence in this file - but it points in a direction that constrains the product thesis rather than flattering it. Evidence THAT diagnosis-driven remediation improves learning: weak to null. Sleeman et al. (1989) found model-based remediation no better than reteaching across three studies; Bunderson & Olsen (1983) found no difference between error-specific remediation and reteaching. Only Swan's (1983) cognitive-conflict approach - confronting the student with the consequences of their error - outperformed reteaching, which localises the active ingredient in the CONFRONTATION ACTIVITY rather than in the diagnosis. Evidence THAT the surrounding tutoring system improves learning: strong. Cognitive Tutor Algebra I produced a statistically significant +0.21 SD in high schools in the SECOND year of implementation (equivalent to moving a median student from the 50th to roughly the 58th percentile), with a similar but non-significant estimate for middle schools and NO effect at all in year one - implementation maturity mattered as much as the software. ASSISTments produced g = 0.18 in Maine, replicated in North Carolina with roughly 0.10 persisting a year post-intervention, with larger benefits for lower-prior-achievement students and for minority students, and WestEd's cost-effectiveness analysis found the cost per student very low. The Prihar et al. (2022) 50-experiment aggregation found five of six tutoring strategies significantly better than 'answer only'. VanLehn (2011) puts step-based tutoring at d = 0.76 versus answer-based at d = 0.31. Net reading for a B/K/H product: the diagnosis is the ENABLER, not the intervention. Budget accordingly - the remediation activities, the cognitive-conflict tasks, the contrasting cases and the follow-up practice are where the effect size is bought, and a product that invests 90% of its effort in the diagnostic engine and 10% in what happens after the diagnosis is inverting the evidence. Also note the year-one null in the RAND trial as a realistic expectation-setter: even a well-built system produced nothing measurable until adults had a year to learn to use it.

### Riset frontier AI misconception-detection 2025-2026 (EDGE framework, 'Correct Answer Trap')

<a id="riset-frontier-ai-misconception-detection-2025-2026-edge-framework-correct-answer-trap"></a>
**Basic Info**

- **name**: > Frontier AI misconception-detection research 2025-2026: the 'Correct Answer Trap' (Imran & Bulathwela), the EDGE framework (Verma), and adjacent step-trace work (MalruleLib, 'Making Evidence Actionable')
- **category**: academic_framework
- **origin_region**: > Global, UK-centred for the empirical core. The two Correct Answer Trap papers are by Moiz Imran and Sahan Bulathwela, working with data from Eedi, a UK edtech company whose diagnostic-question platform serves 250,000+ teachers; Bulathwela is associated with University College London's AI-for-education group and the papers target AIED'26 (International Conference on Artificial Intelligence in Education). The EDGE framework is a single-author theoretical preprint by Ananda Prakash Verma. MalruleLib comes from a US group (Xinghe Chen, Naiming Liu, Shashank Sonkar - the Rice University / educational-NLP lineage). 'Making Evidence Actionable in Adaptive Learning' is by Amirreza Mehrabi, Jason W. Morphew, Breejha Quezada and N. Sanjay Rebello, US university physics education. No Indonesian authorship, no Indonesian data, and no Southeast Asian replication in any of them.
- **delivery_mode**: > Not a product. These are open-access research artifacts: arXiv preprints, one with a Springer chapter (AIED'26 proceedings), plus a released dataset/benchmark in the MalruleLib case. There is no app, no subscription, no deployment. Their relevance is as evidence and as design constraint, not as competition. The nearest thing to a deployable proposal is the 'detect-verify-escalate pipeline' sketched in the June 2026 Correct Answer Trap paper, which is a described architecture rather than a shipped system.
- **language_localization**: > English only, in every artifact. Eedi's question bank and student explanations are English (UK); MalruleLib's generated step traces are English; EDGE's pseudocode is language-neutral but its worked examples are English. There is no Bahasa Indonesia evaluation of any of these findings, and no non-English replication was found. This is itself a research gap worth naming in the thesis: the correct-answer-trap detection rates reported here are a CEILING measured under the most favourable linguistic conditions available to an LLM, and there is no published reason to assume they hold for Indonesian-language student explanations, where model performance on reasoning tasks is generally weaker.
- **local_curriculum_alignment**: > None. Eedi's diagnostic questions are aligned to the English national curriculum; MalruleLib is grounded in US/international mathematics-education literature on documented misconceptions. There is no Kurikulum Merdeka or K13 mapping. What transfers is the METHODOLOGICAL warning and the evaluation design, not the item bank or the misconception list - and the Payne-and-Squibb-style caution about bug libraries not transferring across populations applies with full force to importing these misconception taxonomies into Indonesian classrooms.

**Diagnosis Capability**

- **does_error_type_diagnosis**: > yes - error-type diagnosis is precisely the object of study, and the contribution of this literature is to measure how badly it fails in a specific, predictable, and previously under-reported way. The Eedi paradigm is distractor-mapped: each multiple-choice diagnostic question has distractors that each embody a named misconception, so choosing option C IS a misconception label. On top of that, the dataset used in the Correct Answer Trap papers pairs each response with the student's own free-text EXPLANATION of their reasoning, which is what makes it possible to ask the key question: when the student's final answer is correct, does the model still notice that the METHOD was wrong? The task is framed as a classification with distinct cases - True-Correct (right answer, right reasoning) versus True-Misconception (right answer, WRONG reasoning) - and it is on the True-Misconception class that everything falls apart.
- **diagnosis_granularity**: > mcq-distractor-mapped-misconception, extended by free-text reasoning classification. Eedi's diagnostic questions map each wrong option to a specific misconception, giving a named-misconception taxonomy rather than a score. The Correct Answer Trap work adds a second, orthogonal axis that is the genuinely novel contribution and that a B/K/H product should copy directly: SEPARATING ANSWER CORRECTNESS FROM METHOD VALIDITY. The June 2026 follow-up paper proposes a 'graduated assessment rubric' that does exactly this - it refuses to let 'the answer is right' collapse into 'the reasoning is right'. MalruleLib pushes granularity further still, to executable STEP TRACES: 101 mal-rules turned into runnable procedures that generate the full step-by-step working a student applying that misconception would produce, across 498 parameterised problem templates and over one million generated instances. EDGE's Diagnose stage operates at posterior-probability-over-misconceptions granularity, inferred from distractor patterns and response latencies. Notably, none of these reaches a small parent-legible causal taxonomy of the B/K/H kind - they produce either named item-specific misconceptions (many, technical) or probability distributions (continuous, uninterpretable to a parent).
- **diagnosis_input_modality**: > mcq-distractor plus TYPED free-text student explanation - explicitly NOT handwriting, and the gap this leaves is the strongest positive argument in the whole file for a handwriting-based product. The Eedi dataset records, per response: the question, the correct answer, the student's chosen answer, and the student's typed explanation of their reasoning. That typed explanation is what lets a model catch a correct-answer-wrong-method case at all - without it, detection is structurally impossible, because the only observable (the final answer) is correct. MalruleLib works with synthetic step traces rather than authentic student handwriting. EDGE uses distractor patterns and response latencies, i.e. no reasoning artifact at all. Nobody in this literature ingests a photograph of a child's exercise book. The chain of implication for a B/K/H design is tight: (1) final-answer-only diagnosis cannot detect this failure class even in principle; (2) getting the reasoning artifact is therefore necessary; (3) these papers get it by making students TYPE an explanation, which is a heavy compliance burden and, for an Indonesian 9-year-old on a borrowed phone, essentially unachievable; (4) handwritten working is the reasoning artifact children already produce voluntarily and for free, so capturing it is a cheaper route to the same evidence - but no published system does this, and the OCR-of-messy-child-handwriting problem is unsolved and unmeasured in this literature. That is simultaneously the opportunity and the technical risk.
- **ai_tutor_diagnosis_reliability**: > This is the headline, and the widely circulated framing of it is wrong in a way that matters. The correct numbers, from Imran & Bulathwela, 'Catching The Correct Answer Trap: Characterising AI Tutor Blind Spots When Analysing Student Reasoning' (arXiv 2605.23925, submitted 20 April 2026, AIED'26): on True-Misconception cases - right answer reached by wrong reasoning - recall was T5-small 57.4%, BERT-base 54.1%, Llama-3.1-8B 83.6%, Llama-3.3-70B 81.6%, and Gemini 3 Flash 83.6% (with 94.0% recall on True-Correct cases by contrast). So the 57-84% band is NOT 'even frontier LLMs only catch 57-84%'; it is the spread from small fine-tuned classifiers at the bottom (57%) to frontier and open-weight LLMs at the top (about 84%). The accurate claim is: the best model tested still MISSES ROUGHLY ONE IN SIX hidden misconceptions, and cheap locally-runnable models miss nearly half. Four further findings sharpen this. (1) The failure is CONCENTRATED, not diffuse: 71% of all True-Misconception cases cluster in just two question types that share a structure where a common wrong method happens to yield the right number - which means the failure is PREDICTABLE AT ITEM-DESIGN TIME, and the paper's own recommendation is that question authors check whether common errors produce correct answers for the chosen numeric values. (2) The base rate is brutal: True-Misconception cases are only about 2% of the Eedi dataset, so even good recall drowns in false positives - the paper reports roughly FOUR FALSE ALARMS PER GENUINE DETECTION with the best model, and the June 2026 follow-up puts the false-alarm ratio at approximately 8:1 at realistic prevalence. (3) The mechanism is a learned shortcut: models default to 'correct answer implies correct reasoning', and fine-tuned models learn near-perfect answer-checking while remaining unable to distinguish valid from invalid reasoning when the answer is correct. Fine-tuning on this data makes the shortcut WORSE, not better. (4) High headline accuracy hides all of this - the authors' own summary is that 'high overall accuracy can mask critical failures in reasoning assessment'. Their conclusion is that fully automated screening is impractical at these false-alarm rates and that 'careful analysis of student reasoning still benefits from human judgment'. Cross-cutting caveat on reliability of the FINDING itself: it rests on a single platform's data and only 15 unique questions, so the specific percentages should be treated as indicative rather than as stable population parameters.
- **evidence_quote**: > From the abstract of arXiv 2605.23925: 'Intelligent tutoring systems increasingly provide automated feedback on student work, but robust feedback requires assessing reasoning, not only final answers. We study a failure mode we call the correct answer trap (CAT): models under-detect misconceptions when students reach a correct answer via flawed reasoning.' The worked example that makes the phenomenon concrete: a student solving (-8) - (-5) writes 'I did 8 minus 5, which is 3, and since there are negatives the answer is -3' - reaching the correct answer via a flawed rule (subtract the smaller absolute value from the larger, then assign a negative sign). From the June 2026 follow-up (arXiv 2606.23205): using 20,964 real student responses, 'fine-tuned classifiers detect only 57% of misconceptions, while an open-weight reasoning model achieves 84% detection', with a false-alarm ratio of roughly 8:1 at realistic prevalence. On the diagnosis-without-remediation gap, from Mehrabi et al. (arXiv 2511.14052): adaptive learning systems suffer from 'diagnoses precise yet interventions weak'. And on the value of step-level evidence, from MalruleLib (arXiv 2601.03217): 'providing student step traces improves prediction by 3-15%' over final-answer-only diagnosis.

**Parent Role**

- **how_parent_involved**: > none. Parents appear nowhere in this literature. The two deployment modes the Correct Answer Trap follow-up contemplates are TEACHER DASHBOARDS and AUTONOMOUS AI TUTORS - the human-in-the-loop it assumes is a teacher, and its detect-verify-escalate pipeline explicitly routes uncertain cases to diagnostic follow-up QUESTIONS for the student rather than to a human adult, partly to avoid flooding teachers with false alarms. EDGE's governance layer is likewise instructor-facing, and Mehrabi et al.'s mechanism is explicitly 'instructor-governed'. No paper models a non-expert adult as the recipient of a diagnosis, and none addresses the question of what a diagnosis must look like to be actionable by someone who cannot do the mathematics themselves. For an Indonesian home product where the only available adult is a parent with no teaching training, this literature offers the detection science and nothing whatsoever about the delivery.
- **ai_tutor_directly_to_child**: > partial, and deliberately hedged - which is itself the most transferable design recommendation here. The papers do NOT recommend letting an AI tutor act autonomously on its own misconception detections, because the false-alarm rate (4:1, or 8:1 at realistic prevalence) would mean the child is repeatedly told they have a misconception they do not have. Instead the proposed architecture is detect-verify-escalate: detect a possible hidden misconception, then VERIFY it by routing the child to a targeted diagnostic follow-up question rather than immediately delivering a correction, and only escalate to a human when uncertainty persists. This is a genuinely good pattern to steal for a B/K/H product: never assert a diagnosis to a child or parent on a single observation; confirm it with a cheap, purpose-built probe item first. It also dovetails with the older Cognitive Tutor evidence, where confronting the student with the consequences of their rule (cognitive conflict) outperformed simply naming the error - a well-chosen verification probe is a cognitive-conflict task and a confirmation test at the same time.

**Evidence & Market Signal**

- **key_finding**: > Three findings, plus two factual corrections to the framing of this research request. CORRECTION ONE: the '57-84%' figures are a range ACROSS MODEL CLASSES, not a frontier-model ceiling. T5-small gets 57.4% and Gemini 3 Flash gets 83.6% on hidden-misconception recall. The defensible claim is 'the best available models still miss about one in six hidden misconceptions, and the cheap models you could actually afford to run at classroom scale miss nearly half' - which is a stronger and more honest argument than the inflated version, because it exposes a COST-ACCURACY frontier: the accuracy exists only at a price point that the papers themselves call prohibitive for routine use. CORRECTION TWO: arXiv 2511.14052 is NOT an EDGE paper. It is 'Making Evidence Actionable in Adaptive Learning' by Mehrabi, Morphew, Quezada & Rebello - and it happens to be MORE useful to this thesis than the paper it was mistaken for, because its entire premise is the gap between 'diagnoses precise' and 'interventions weak'. It proposes an instructor-governed mechanism converting assessment evidence into targeted micro-interventions under three constraints (adequacy, attention, diversity), formalises intervention assignment as an optimisation problem, and tests it on 1,204 introductory physics students, achieving full skill coverage within bounded study time. That is a concrete template for the remediation half of a B/K/H product, and it independently corroborates the thirty-five-year-old Sleeman finding that diagnosis without a well-designed intervention is inert. The actual EDGE paper is arXiv 2508.07224, 'EDGE: A Theoretical Framework for Misconception-Aware Adaptive Learning' by Ananda Prakash Verma (10 August 2025), a four-stage Evaluate-Diagnose-Generate-Exercise framework with an EdgeScore readiness metric, proofs of monotonicity and Lipschitz continuity, and a restless-bandit scheduling policy - but it must be cited with a large caveat, because the paper states outright that 'empirical study is left to future work'. It is mathematics about learning, with zero students in it. Its one genuinely useful idea for remediation design is CONTRASTIVE / COUNTERFACTUAL ITEM GENERATION: minimally perturbed items that invalidate a learner's shortcut while preserving psychometric validity, for which the paper establishes conditions under which such items provably reduce the posterior probability of a targeted misconception faster than standard practice. That is the modern formalisation of Swan's cognitive-conflict remediation, and it is exactly the remediation activity the Cognitive Tutor evidence says actually works. NOW THE THREE SUBSTANTIVE FINDINGS. FIRST, final-answer-only diagnosis is not merely imprecise, it is STRUCTURALLY BLIND to an entire error class. When a flawed method yields a correct number there is no observable signal in the answer at all; detection is impossible in principle without a reasoning artifact. This is the cleanest possible refutation of every competitor in the Indonesian market whose 'diagnosis' is built on scoring final answers or on MCQ selection alone. SECOND, the failure is structured and therefore exploitable: 71% of hidden-misconception cases concentrate in two question types with a shared numeric structure, which means an item author can predict and either avoid or deliberately target these traps at design time by checking whether a common wrong method yields the right answer for the chosen values. A B/K/H item bank should do this deliberately - construct diagnostic items where the common misconception and the correct method DIVERGE numerically, so the answer itself carries diagnostic signal, and reserve expensive step-analysis for the residual cases. That is a cheap engineering win nobody in the Indonesian market is taking. THIRD, and most favourable to the handwriting thesis: MalruleLib (arXiv 2601.03217, 6 January 2026) measured the value of step traces directly and found that giving models the student's intermediate STEPS improves misconception prediction by 3-15 percentage points over final-answer-only, across nine models from 4B to 120B parameters. It also found the harder truth - cross-template misconception prediction sits at only about 40%, degrading 10-21 points when the problem template changes, so a model that has learned to spot a misconception in one problem shape largely fails to spot the same misconception in another. Combined with VanLehn's older finding that step-based tutoring reaches d = 0.76 versus answer-based at d = 0.31, the case for capturing handwritten working is now supported from two independent directions, thirteen years apart. SYNTHESISED IMPLICATION FOR A B/K/H PRODUCT: the gap is real and is not closing on its own - frontier research in 2026 still cannot reliably diagnose reasoning from answers, and explicitly recommends keeping a human in the loop. But the same research constrains the product hard: (a) never diagnose from final answers alone; (b) capture the step-level reasoning artifact, and handwriting is the only version of that artifact an Indonesian SD child will produce voluntarily; (c) expect to miss roughly one in six even with the best models, so build for graceful uncertainty rather than confident labels; (d) the false-positive problem is worse than the false-negative problem at low base rates - design a verification probe before ever telling a parent their child has a misconception; (e) separate 'answer correct' from 'method valid' as two explicitly different fields in your data model and in your parent report, because collapsing them is the exact bug that defeats frontier models; and (f) pair every confirmed diagnosis with a contrastive/counterfactual item that makes the child's rule visibly break, since that is the one remediation form with both a theoretical argument (EDGE) and old empirical support (Swan's conflict approach) behind it.
- **study_scale_or_sample**: > Modest, and this should temper how hard the numbers are pushed. Correct Answer Trap (arXiv 2605.23925 and its June 2026 follow-up 2606.23205): 20,964 real student responses from the Eedi mathematics platform - a platform serving over 250,000 teachers - but spanning only 15 UNIQUE QUESTIONS, classified as procedural, conceptual or mixed. Each response carries the question, correct answer, student answer and student explanation. True-Misconception cases are only about 2% of the dataset, and 71% of those concentrate in the top two question types, so the number of independent observations underpinning the headline recall figures is far smaller than 20,964 suggests. Five models evaluated: T5-small, BERT-base, Gemini 3 Flash, Llama-3.3-70B, Llama-3.1-8B. MalruleLib (arXiv 2601.03217): 101 mal-rules, 498 parameterised problem templates, over one million generated instances, nine models from 4B to 120B parameters - but the instances are SYNTHETIC, generated by executing mal-rules, not collected from real children. EDGE (arXiv 2508.07224): zero empirical data; theory and pseudocode only, with empirical study explicitly deferred. Mehrabi et al. (arXiv 2511.14052): 1,204 students in an introductory university physics course - real students, real intervention, but wrong subject and wrong age for direct transfer. Background context for the Eedi paradigm: the NeurIPS 2020 Education Challenge dataset released by Eedi contained 125,000 students, 28,000 questions and 20 million answers across two school years, covering roughly ages 7-18.
- **evidence_strength**: > moderate - genuinely useful, but weaker than the numbers' rhetorical force suggests, and the thesis should say so rather than overclaim. Reasons for moderate rather than strong: (1) the Correct Answer Trap results come from a single platform's data and only 15 unique questions, with the critical True-Misconception class at about 2% prevalence, so the specific recall percentages are indicative not definitive; (2) both CAT papers are by the same two authors on the same dataset, so the 'two complementary papers' are one research programme, not independent replication - the June 2026 paper rounds 57.4% to 57% and 83.6% to 84% and reports a different false-alarm ratio (8:1 versus roughly 4:1) under different prevalence assumptions, which is a reframing of the same evidence, not corroboration; (3) at least one is a preprint not yet through peer review, though the April 2026 paper is accepted to AIED'26 and has a Springer chapter, which raises its standing considerably; (4) EDGE is entirely unvalidated by its own admission and should never be cited as evidence of anything, only as a source of framework vocabulary and one formal argument about contrastive items; (5) MalruleLib's step-trace advantage is measured on SYNTHETIC traces, so the 3-15 point gain is an upper bound for what real, messy, incomplete child handwriting would yield; (6) no result here is replicated outside English, and none touches Indonesian learners or Indonesian-language reasoning. What IS strong: the QUALITATIVE claim that final-answer-only assessment is structurally blind to correct-answer-wrong-method cases. That does not depend on any percentage - it is a logical consequence of the observable, and it holds regardless of how the recall numbers shake out on a larger sample. Build the thesis argument on the structural claim and use the percentages as illustration, not as load-bearing evidence.

### Riset stroke/handwriting analytics untuk diagnosis matematika

<a id="riset-strokehandwriting-analytics-untuk-diagnosis-matematika"></a>
**Basic Info**

- **name**: Stroke / Handwriting Analytics for Mathematics Diagnosis (digital-ink AIED & HCI research precedent)
- **category**: academic_framework
- **origin_region**: > Global, with four distinct research clusters: (1) Japan — Kyoto University LEAF/BookRoll/LAViEW platform (Nakamoto, Flanagan, Dai, Takami, Ogata), the most math-specific and most mature; (2) USA — CMU HCII pen-based algebra tutors (Anthony, Yang, Koedinger) and Oviatt's multimodal learning analytics on the ICMI Math Data Corpus; (3) Hong Kong — HKUST (Sheng, Shen, Duan, Shen, Jin, Hui), AIED 2026 stage-aware handwriting-trajectory framework; (4) Europe — Germany (primary-school pen-pressure trace studies; DFKI digital-pen clinical scoring) and EU/MENA dysgraphia-screening literature. No Indonesian or Southeast Asian study located.
- **delivery_mode**: > online-app (tablet plus active stylus) deployed as research prototypes inside real classrooms — effectively hybrid: digital capture, but the deployment and the ground-truth labeling remain teacher/researcher-mediated. Kyoto's LEAF stack (BookRoll for handwriting capture, LAViEW for analytics) ran month-long and multi-year in-school deployments; the German primary-school studies ran in authentic classroom sessions. None of these are consumer apps.
- **pricing_model**: > Not applicable — academic research, not a commercial product. Underlying platforms are institutional and free-to-research (LEAF/BookRoll/LAViEW at Kyoto University); published proceedings are mostly paywalled (Springer LNCS), while the fullest methodological paper (Nakamoto et al. 2025, RPTEL) is open access CC-BY.

**Diagnosis Capability**

- **does_error_type_diagnosis**: > partial. The literature reliably localizes WHERE a learner breaks down (which problem-solving step, which moment in the stroke timeline, whether an impasse occurred) and characterizes HOW they worked (planning style, effort, expertise level, cognitive load). It does not, in any published work found, classify WHY into a semantic error taxonomy such as reading-error / conceptual-error / computational-error. Nakamoto et al. produce a binary impasse-or-not label per rubric step; Sheng et al. (AIED 2026) produce behavioral learner profiles and regulatory-event features, not error causes; Oviatt produces an expertise classification with no content analysis at all. Stroke analytics is therefore best read as an impasse-localization and effort-profiling layer that must be paired with a separate semantic error-classification layer to yield a B/K/H-style diagnosis.
- **diagnosis_granularity**: > Between trouble-spot-quantity and topic-mastery-map, and short of full-error-type-taxonomy. The most granular published output is per-step binary impasse labels against a hand-authored 4-step rubric of knowledge components (K1-K4) for each problem, temporally anchored to specific stroke numbers in the handwriting replay (e.g. 'Stroke 34: calculated area of triangle ABC — correct step; Stroke 201: expressed coordinates of Q in variables — incorrect step'). AIED 2026 adds a coarser layer: stage-aware regulatory events, question-level dynamics, and stable per-learner behavioral profiles. Nothing in the reviewed corpus emits a named error type.
- **diagnosis_input_modality**: > handwriting-stroke, and in the strongest studies handwriting-stroke fused with a second channel. Captured signals across the literature: pen-tip x/y coordinates and velocity vectors, stroke order and count, stroke duration and distance, pen pressure, azimuth/altitude (tilt), pen-up/pen-down ratio and in-air time, inter-stroke pauses and pause duration, total vs. active activity duration ('accurate activity duration', excluding idle intervals), and discrete correction events logged by the platform (UNDO_HW_MEMO, REDO_HW_MEMO, CLEAR_HW_MEMO, DELETE_MEMO, CHANGE_MEMO, PAGE_JUMP). Kyoto fuses these with typed self-explanations embedded via BERT/SBERT and cosine-matched to gold-standard step explanations; HKUST fuses raw trajectories with a Multimodal LLM used as an interpretive layer mapping low-level strokes to functional cognitive operations. Practical constraint for a low-cost Android build: pressure and tilt — the features Oviatt found most predictive — require an active stylus and are unavailable with finger or passive capacitive input; timing, pause, stroke-count, geometry and correction-event features survive finger input.
- **ai_tutor_diagnosis_reliability**: > Partially validated against human ground truth, with clearly documented and directly relevant failure modes. (1) Best published accuracy for math impasse detection is 80.06% (Nakamoto et al. 2025, hierarchical logistic regression, 900 answers from 93 students across 23 quizzes, 2020-2022), against a human-labeled ground truth in which evaluators reviewed both the handwritten answer and the aligned self-explanations and marked each rubric step correct or incorrect. (2) Critically, the stroke channel is the WEAKER half: a self-explanation-only model already reached 74.0%; adding all handwriting and raw-operational features gained only ~6 points. Digital ink alone is not established as sufficient for step-level diagnosis. (3) The 'correct answer trap' analog is explicitly acknowledged rather than solved: the study adopts VanLehn's definition in which an impasse manifests as being stuck, detecting an error, or doubt 'even when they are technically correct in their actions', and states the approach 'extends beyond the conventional binary evaluation of responses as merely correct or incorrect' to probe students who 'rely on rote memorization or have a cursory understanding'. (4) The authors' own stated limitation is inverse-signal risk: they assume good self-explanations mean no barrier and poor ones signal a problem, but 'not all students are equally skilled at explaining their thoughts in writing, which can lead to misjudging their understanding' — a severe risk for 9-10 year olds with limited writing fluency. (5) Oviatt (ACM TiiS 2018) found average stroke distance, pressure and duration adapt significantly with expertise but writing speed does NOT — a warning that the most intuitive feature (speed) is the wrong one. (6) Handwriting recognition itself is unreliable; Anthony/Yang/Koedinger describe recognition error as 'a clear downside to introducing handwriting input into intelligent tutors', with domain adaptation reducing errors by only 18%. (7) The adjacent dysgraphia-screening literature (Fallah et al., PLoS One 2025, 21 studies) reports 73-99% accuracy but flags small sample sizes, language-specific models, heterogeneous metrics, and that lab performance does not consistently transfer to real settings.
- **evidence_quote**: > Nakamoto et al. (2025, RPTEL 20:19): 'Our analysis combines various data types, including handwritten responses and digital self-explanations from 93 middle school students. Using hierarchical logistic regression, we examined feature groups such as Self-Explanation Quality, Handwriting Features, and Overall Level of Action. Models based solely on self-explanation achieved a 74.0% accuracy rate, while adding more features increased the final model's accuracy to 80.06%.' — and on granularity: 'BookRoll captures the handwriting data as a series of vectors representing the coordinates and velocity of pen strokes... This data proves valuable for understanding stroke order, pauses, and handwriting patterns, aiding in anomaly detection.' Sheng et al. (AIED 2026): the framework 'transforms raw handwriting trajectories from digital pens into a multi-level behavioral representation, leveraging Multimodal Large Language Models as an interpretive layer to map low-level pen strokes to functional cognitive operations', because outcome-based correctness signals give 'limited insight into how students regulate their problem-solving'.

**Parent Role**

- **ai_tutor_directly_to_child**: > yes, but thin and school-mediated. The Kyoto EXAIT/stuck-point system automatically detects stuck points and recommends related problems as personalized feedback directly to the learner, and prompts the child to self-explain by replaying their own handwriting timeline and annotating points in time — a direct child-facing interaction loop. It is a recommender plus reflection prompt, not a conversational tutor that explains a misconception in words. No reviewed system has an AI that talks to the child about WHY the error happened.

**Evidence & Market Signal**

- **key_finding**: > Digital-ink stroke analytics is a real, peer-reviewed academic precedent for going 'beyond correctness' in mathematics — but it validates impasse LOCALIZATION, not error-type CLASSIFICATION, and it has never been demonstrated at grade-4 arithmetic. Five findings matter for the B/K/H handwriting-input thesis: (1) The approach is legitimate and current — AIED 2026 (Seoul) accepted a full paper whose entire premise is that correctness signals give 'limited insight into how students regulate their problem-solving', and Kyoto University has a multi-year classroom deployment stack capturing coordinates, velocity, stroke order, pauses and undo/redo/erase events. (2) The ceiling is modest and stroke data is the junior partner: 80.06% accuracy on binary per-step impasse detection, of which self-explanation TEXT alone already delivered 74.0%. Betting a diagnosis engine on strokes alone contradicts the best available evidence; the durable pattern is stroke-timeline as the temporal anchor for a semantic channel (self-explanation text, or the answer content itself). (3) The gap is precisely where the OSN product sits: nobody has mapped stroke features onto a reading/concept/calculation error taxonomy, nobody has done math-process stroke diagnosis with primary-school children (the grade 3-4 pen studies infer cognitive load and emotional valence, not math errors), and no commercial edtech product was found doing stroke-level error-type diagnosis — Mathpix and similar vendors sell handwriting-to-LaTeX recognition, which is transcription, not diagnosis. (4) Hardware reality check for a borrowed low-cost Android phone: the features with the strongest published support are pressure, stroke distance and stroke duration (Oviatt: pressure and distance adapt with expertise, writing SPEED does not), and pressure/tilt need an active stylus. A finger-input phone retains timing, pause-length, stroke-count, correction/erase-event and geometry features — the German primary study's headline signal, pen pressure, would not be available. (5) There is a validated cheaper proxy already in the literature: platform-logged discrete correction events (UNDO/REDO/CLEAR/DELETE memo, page jumps) and duration segments were part of the winning feature set and require no pen sensor at all — arguably the highest-value, lowest-cost signal for detecting a 'Hitung' slip versus a 'Konsep' stall on commodity hardware.
- **evidence_strength**: moderate
- **remediation_efficacy_evidence**: > Weak to absent — this is the sharpest hole in the entire literature. No RCT or quasi-experiment was found showing that stroke-derived or process-derived diagnosis actually improves mathematics learning outcomes. The closest study, the AIED 2025 Kyoto pilot with ~80 middle-schoolers over roughly one month, reports only that the system 'was capable of automatically detecting stuck points from self-explanations and recommending related problems as personalized feedback' — a feasibility and detection result, with no reported learning gain against a control. Nakamoto et al. (2025) is purely a detection-accuracy study. Oviatt's expertise-prediction work is correlational. The one genuine controlled comparison in the adjacent space concerns input modality rather than diagnosis: Anthony, Yang & Koedinger found students solved algebra problems roughly twice as fast with handwriting than with typing, made fewer entry errors, showed increased transfer to non-computer tasks, and 'seem to learn just as much as their typing counterparts' — i.e. handwriting input is a defensible UX choice with an efficiency benefit, but that is not evidence that stroke-based DIAGNOSIS remediates anything. Separately, a large RCT does exist for LLM-generated feedback helping low-knowledge middle-school math students with short-term learning (ACM L@S 2026), suggesting the feedback-delivery half of the loop has independent support even though the stroke-diagnosis half does not.

**Diagnosis Capability**

- **does_error_type_diagnosis**: > no — at the market level, nothing in the Indonesian mainstream (schools, bimbel, or apps) diagnoses error TYPE. (1) SCHOOL: the Kurikulum Merdeka rapor produces narrative descriptors ending in phrases like 'Perlu bimbingan lebih lanjut' / 'Perlu mendapatkan bimbingan lagi untuk menemukan solusi' — a verdict without a cause. Kumon reduces mastery to accuracy plus Standard Completion Time; Sparks Math places by 'kemampuan aktual' and separately offers a 'Test Gaya Belajar Anak' (learning-style test), which is a learner-trait instrument, not an error-diagnostic one. (3) APPS: topic-mastery mapping at best. The single most telling artefact is that Sparks Math's own marketing content NAMES the three-way error taxonomy in prose — 'salah memahami soal, salah menerapkan rumus, atau kesalahan hitung yang tidak disengaja' — while shipping no instrument that measures it. The vocabulary of the gap already exists in Indonesian commercial copy; the measurement does not.
- **diagnosis_granularity**: > score-only at the school layer (narrative descriptors plus a numeric range), level-placement at the premium offline layer (ability-based placement test producing a starting point in a fixed worksheet/skill sequence), and topic-mastery-map at the strongest app layer. Nothing observed in the Indonesian market reaches mcq-distractor-mapped-misconception, let alone full-error-type-taxonomy. The national AKM/Asesmen Nasional layer is coarser still: it is reported as a single binary per domain — proportion of students reaching versus not reaching 'kompetensi minimum numerasi' — which is a one-bit diagnosis applied to a census of ~24 million SD students.
- **diagnosis_input_modality**: > Predominantly final-answer scoring: mcq and short-answer marked right/wrong. Offline providers are the only actors that routinely collect the child's handwritten working (Kumon collects step-by-step worksheets daily; CoLearn collects weekly non-multiple-choice handwritten exercises; Sparks Math runs in-person small groups of 10-15 where an instructor sees the work), but in every case that working is consumed by a human grader's eye and reduced to a score, a completion time, or an error count — never captured as classified data. This is the market's central waste: the richest diagnostic substrate in Indonesia is already being physically collected at scale and thrown away at the point of conversion into a parent report. No Indonesian provider observed uses handwriting-stroke capture or free-response OCR of the child's own attempt for diagnostic classification.
- **evidence_quote**: > Three quotes carry the claim. (1) THE PARENT CANNOT READ THE VERDICT — from BIC's guide to Kurikulum Merdeka learning phases, a father described reading his child's report card that has no red marks, no ranking, only long descriptions ending in 'Perlu bimbingan lebih lanjut' (needs further guidance), asks: 'Anak saya ini pintar atau tidak, sih? Kok bahasanya muter-muter?' ('Is my child actually smart or not? Why does the language just go round in circles?'). (2) THE PARENT CANNOT EXPLAIN THE FAILURE DESPITE PAYING FOR EFFORT — from Sparks Math's own blog on why Kelas 6 children fail maths exams: 'Yang membuat banyak orang tua bingung adalah kenyataan bahwa anak mereka sudah belajar keras, sudah mengerjakan banyak latihan soal, tapi hasil ujiannya tetap tidak sesuai harapan.' ('What confuses many parents is the reality that their child has already studied hard and done many practice problems, yet the exam results still fall short of expectations.') (3) THE TAXONOMY IS NAMED BUT NOT MEASURED — the same Sparks Math article decomposes practice errors into 'salah memahami soal, salah menerapkan rumus, atau kesalahan hitung yang tidak disengaja' ('misunderstanding the problem, applying the wrong formula, or unintentional calculation mistakes') — a near-exact restatement of the B/K/H triad (Baca/Konsep/Hitung) appearing in Indonesian commercial marketing copy — while the only assessment the company actually offers the visitor is a 'Test Gaya Belajar Anak' (child's learning-style test). Naming the error types is already a selling proposition in Indonesia; measuring them is not yet a product.

**Parent Role**

- **how_parent_involved**: > gatekeeper + dashboard-consumer, with a documented willingness to spend both money and physical time on the reporting ritual itself. The parent is the payer and the installer of the app on an often-shared phone, and in the offline channel the parent is also an active participant: Kumon Indonesia enrolment begins with the parent booking an instructor appointment, attending a free learning consultation, then attending monthly parent orientation (orientasi orang tua) and supervising the daily at-home worksheet routine; Sparks Math schedules progress sheets and parent-teacher meetings at 1 and 3 months with further direct consultation at months 3 and 6. Online players push reports to the parent's WhatsApp (Ruangguru's monthly Laporan Belajar retrievable by texting 'Rapor Anak'; CoLearn's attendance and exam-readiness reports 'Langsung ke WhatsApp orang tua'). The uniform limitation across all of them is that what the parent receives is scores, topic names, attendance, completion times and error COUNTS — never an error TYPE, and never a script for what to do differently at home. The parent is positioned to observe and to pay, never to coach.

**Evidence & Market Signal**

- **study_scale_or_sample**: > This item aggregates four bodies of evidence of very different weight. (1) PISA 2022 Indonesia — nationally representative sample survey: 13,439 students in 410 schools completed the mathematics, reading or science assessment, representing about 3,790,800 15-year-olds, roughly 85% of Indonesia's total 15-year-old population; conducted by OECD with standard sampling and weighting, so the 366 mathematics mean and the 18%-at-Level-2 figure are the strongest numbers in this record. (2) AKM / Asesmen Nasional & Rapor Pendidikan 2022-2024 — a national CENSUS rather than a sample, covering the full SD/SMP/SMA population (SD alone ~23.93 million students in the 2024/2025 odd semester), which is why the numeracy series (45.24% / 62.45% / 67.94%) is the highest-coverage evidence available, offset by extremely low granularity (one binary verdict per domain). (3) Provider pricing and placement evidence — a purposive sample of six named providers (Sparks Math, Kumon Indonesia, Sakamoto, Ruangguru Math Champs, CoLearn, Superprof), taken from official sites, published fee schedules effective 1 January 2026, and one provider-authored price-comparison article; commercially self-reported and therefore promotional in tone, though the Kumon fee schedule is an official published tariff. (4) Parent-complaint evidence — the weakest strand, and the honest limitation of this record: it rests on illustrative narrative vignettes in Indonesian education media and provider blogs rather than on a survey. No large-n Indonesian survey quantifying parental comprehension of the Kurikulum Merdeka narrative rapor was located; the nearest quantitative proxies are small and off-target (e.g. an IKIP Siliwangi study finding 46.6% of parents understood the 'merdeka' concept, n=30 PAUD parents in Bandung, and a JPTAM study of parental perception with n=10). Market-size figures are third-party commercial estimates (IMARC, Ken Research, 6Wresearch) with undisclosed methodology.
- **evidence_strength**: > moderate overall, but sharply uneven by strand and this unevenness is the key thing to carry forward. STRONG: the macro scale of the numeracy problem (PISA 2022 is a properly sampled international assessment; AKM is a national census) and the price anchors (Kumon's official published fee schedule effective 1 January 2026 is documentary evidence, not a claim). MODERATE: the willingness-to-pay inference — Sparks Math's ability-based placement is verified on the company's own site and its price point is published, and the 3-6x offline-over-online price premium is a real observed gradient, but the causal attribution of that premium specifically to diagnostic precision (rather than to supervision, discipline, physical convenience or brand) is an inference, not a measured elasticity. WEAK / ANECDOTAL: the parent-complaint strand, which is vignette-based (a named parent quote in an education-media article plus a provider blog's framing of parental confusion) and vulnerable to the objection that a tutoring company describing parental confusion is marketing rather than evidence — though the same framing appearing independently in non-commercial education media raises confidence. UNRESOLVED: the market-size figures, where vendor estimates conflict materially and no methodology is disclosed. The single strongest defensible claim in this record is the CONTRAST: nationally measured mass failure (82% of 15-year-olds below Level 2 maths, ~32% of SD students below minimum numeracy) sitting alongside a paid market whose most advanced commercial instrument resolves only LEVEL, not error type.
